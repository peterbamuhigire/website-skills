## BOOK 1: STRATEGIC BRAND MANAGEMENT — Kevin Lane Keller

### Core Definition

> "A brand is a name, term, sign, symbol, or design, or a combination of them, intended to identify the goods and services of one seller or group of sellers and to differentiate them from those of competition." — American Marketing Association

Keller's expansion: A brand is more than that — it has created awareness, reputation, and prominence. The difference between a commodity and a distinctive offering. There is a distinction between a small-b "brand" (the AMA definition: just the identifiers) and a capital-B "Brand" (something with real equity in the market).

**Brand vs. Product — Five Product Levels** (p.69–70):
1. Core benefit level — the fundamental need satisfied
2. Generic product level — basic version with no distinguishing features
3. Expected product level — attributes buyers normally expect
4. Augmented product level — attributes that distinguish from competitors
5. Potential product level — all future augmentations possible

> "A brand is, therefore, more than a product, because it can have dimensions that differentiate it in some way from other products designed to satisfy the same need. These differences may be rational and tangible — related to product performance — or more symbolic, emotional, and intangible — related to what the brand represents." (p.70–71)

### MODEL 1: Customer-Based Brand Equity (CBBE) Framework

**The Central Definition** (Ch. 2, p.185):
> "Customer-based brand equity is the differential effect that brand knowledge has on customer response to the marketing of that brand."

Three key ingredients:
1. **Differential effect** — brand equity arises from differences in customer response
2. **Brand knowledge** — what consumers have learned, felt, seen, heard about the brand over time
3. **Customer response to marketing** — perceptions, preferences, behavior related to all aspects of brand marketing

**The Basic Premise**: The power of a brand lies in what resides in the minds and hearts of customers. All money spent on marketing is an investment in consumer memory structures.

**Two Components of Brand Knowledge**:

**1. Brand Awareness**
- Brand recognition: ability to confirm prior exposure when given the brand as a cue
- Brand recall: ability to retrieve the brand from memory when given a category, need, or usage situation

**2. Brand Image**
- Consumers' perceptions about a brand as reflected by brand associations held in memory
- Associations come in all forms: product attributes, benefits, user imagery, brand personality, feelings

**Sources of Brand Equity** (p.195):
CBBE occurs when a consumer has:
- High level of awareness and familiarity with the brand, AND
- Holds strong, favorable, and unique brand associations in memory

The three dimensions of brand associations:
1. **Strength** — how deeply the information is processed and linked to existing knowledge; personal relevance increases strength
2. **Favorability** — associations that are desirable to customers and successfully delivered by the product
3. **Uniqueness** — associations that provide competitive advantage and reason to buy; cannot be found in competing brands

**Outcomes of Strong Brand Equity** (p.1720–1721):
- Improved perceptions of product performance
- Greater customer loyalty
- Less vulnerability to competitive marketing actions
- Less vulnerability to marketing crises
- Higher margins
- More inelastic consumer response to price increases
- More elastic consumer response to price decreases
- Greater trade cooperation and support
- Increased marketing communication effectiveness
- Possible licensing opportunities
- Additional brand extension opportunities
- Improved attraction and retention of employees

### MODEL 2: Brand Resonance Pyramid (CBBE Pyramid)

**The Four Steps of Brand Building** (Ch. 3, p.302):

The pyramid has six building blocks across four levels, with identity at the base rising to resonance at the top. The left side is the rational route; the right side is the emotional route. Strong brands climb both sides.

```
                    [RESONANCE]
                       /      \
              [JUDGMENTS]    [FEELINGS]
                /                  \
        [PERFORMANCE]          [IMAGERY]
                \                  /
                     [SALIENCE]
```

**Level 1 — SALIENCE (Who are you?)**
Brand awareness depth and breadth. Not just recognition, but the range of purchase and consumption situations where the brand comes to mind. Goal: correct brand identity.

**Level 2 — PERFORMANCE (What are you?)**
How well the product meets customers' functional needs. Five dimensions:
- Primary characteristics and secondary features
- Product reliability, durability, serviceability
- Service effectiveness, efficiency, and empathy
- Style and design
- Price

**Level 2 — IMAGERY (What are you?)**
Extrinsic properties of the product. Four main categories:
- User profiles (who uses the brand)
- Purchase and usage situations
- Personality and values
- History, heritage, experiences

**Level 3 — JUDGMENTS (What about you?)**
Customers' personal opinions and evaluations:
- Brand quality
- Brand credibility (expertise, trustworthiness, likeability)
- Brand consideration (likelihood of being in the consideration set)
- Brand superiority (extent to which customers view it as uniquely better)

**Level 3 — FEELINGS (What about you?)**
Customers' emotional responses and reactions. Six types in ascending intensity:
- Warmth
- Fun
- Excitement
- Security
- Social approval
- Self-respect

**Level 4 — RESONANCE (What about you and me?)**
Ultimate goal: the nature and depth of the psychological bond customers have with the brand.

Four dimensions of resonance:
1. **Behavioral loyalty** — repeat purchase, share of category requirements
2. **Attitudinal attachment** — love the brand, view it as a special possession or little pleasure
3. **Sense of community** — kinship with other brand users; brand becomes social phenomenon
4. **Active engagement** — willingness to invest time, energy, money beyond purchase; brand evangelism

> "Brand resonance is the pinnacle of the brand resonance model and provides important focus and priority for decision making about marketing."

**Key ordering rule**: Cannot establish meaning unless identity exists; responses cannot occur unless meaning is developed; relationship cannot form without proper responses.

### MODEL 3: Brand Positioning

**Definition** (p.210):
> "Brand positioning is the act of designing the company's offer and image so that it occupies a distinct and valued place in the target customer's mind."

**Four Components of Positioning**:
1. Who is the target consumer?
2. Who are the main competitors?
3. How is the brand similar to these competitors? (Points-of-Parity)
4. How is the brand different from them? (Points-of-Difference)

**Points-of-Difference (PODs)**:
Attributes or benefits that consumers strongly associate with a brand, positively evaluate, and believe they could not find to the same extent with a competitive brand. Must be:
- Desirable to consumers
- Deliverable by the company
- Differentiating from competitors

Three criteria for desirability:
- Personal relevance — the benefit matters to the consumer
- Distinctiveness — the brand delivers this better than competitors
- Believability — the brand can credibly claim this

**Points-of-Parity (POPs)**:
Associations that are not necessarily unique to the brand but may be shared with other brands. Three types:
- Category POPs: associations consumers view as necessary to be a legitimate product in the category
- Competitive POPs: associations designed to negate competitors' points-of-difference
- Correlational POPs: associations that may seem negatively related but actually can coexist (e.g., "affordable luxury")

**Frame of Reference**:
Defines the category in which the brand competes. Establishes the basis for comparison. Should be established before identifying PODs and POPs.

**Straddle Positioning**: A brand that straddles two categories (e.g., BMW positioned as both "luxury" and "performance") can create a positioning that encompasses both frames of reference. Risk: if consumers are skeptical, the brand may lose credibility in both categories.

**Positioning Guidelines**:
1. Define and communicate the competitive frame of reference
2. Choose the right PODs — focus on functional benefits rather than just attributes, because attributes are more easily copied; or focus on abstract performance benefits
3. Establish POPs and PODs simultaneously
4. Update positioning over time as the competitive landscape and consumer needs change

### TOOL 1: The Brand Mantra

**Definition** (p.254–255):
> "A brand mantra is a short, three-to-five-word phrase that captures the irrefutable essence or spirit of the brand positioning."

Purpose: Ensures all employees and external partners understand what the brand most fundamentally represents, so they can adjust their actions accordingly.

**Structure of a Brand Mantra** (p.261):

| Component | Nike Example | Disney Example |
|---|---|---|
| Emotional Modifier | Authentic | Fun |
| Descriptive Modifier | Athletic | Family |
| Brand Function | Performance | Entertainment |

- **Brand function**: Describes the nature of the product, service, or experience the brand provides
- **Descriptive modifier**: Clarifies the nature (not just any performance, but athletic performance)
- **Emotional modifier**: How the brand provides its benefits; further distinguishes the brand

**Three Criteria for a Good Brand Mantra** (p.266):
1. **Communicate** — define the category and clarify what the brand is and is not
2. **Simplify** — be memorable, short, vivid, and crisp
3. **Inspire** — personally meaningful and relevant to as many employees as possible

**Practical use**: A brand mantra serves as a "mental filter" and "intellectual guard rails." Any marketing activity inconsistent with the mantra is rejected, no matter how appealing.

### FRAMEWORK 1: Brand Elements

**Definition**: Brand elements (also called brand identities) are trademarkable devices that serve to identify and differentiate the brand. The main ones:
- Brand names
- URLs
- Logos and symbols
- Characters
- Spokespeople
- Slogans
- Jingles
- Packaging
- Signage

**The Test**: What would consumers think or feel about the product if they knew only that particular brand element and nothing else?

**Six Criteria for Choosing Brand Elements** (Ch. 4, p.398):

**Build-oriented criteria (offensive role):**
1. **Memorability** — easily noticed and recalled in purchase or consumption situations
2. **Meaningfulness** — descriptive and persuasive; conveys information about the product category, the product attributes and benefits, or both
3. **Likability** — aesthetically appealing; rich visual and verbal imagery; fun and interesting; can create positive associations through likability alone

**Defensive criteria (protect equity):**
4. **Transferability** — how well the element works to introduce new products in the same or different categories; how much the element adds to brand equity in new geographies or market segments
5. **Adaptability** — how flexible and updateable the element is over time; the more adaptable, the easier to update
6. **Protectability** — how legally and competitively protectable the brand element is; can it be protected legally through trademark registration?

**Brand Name Guidelines** (p.416–425):
- Short names facilitate recall because they are easy to encode
- Simple and easy to pronounce or spell
- Familiar and meaningful
- Different, distinctive, and unusual
- Types: descriptive names, suggestive names, compound names, classical names (Latin/Greek roots), arbitrary names, fanciful names

**Slogan Best Practices**:
- Short phrases that communicate descriptive or persuasive information
- Can function as a hook or handle to help consumers grasp the brand's positioning
- Unlike brand names, slogans can be changed when they become outdated
- Best slogans are a seamless part of brand advertising that consumers can recall without the brand name

### PROCESS 1: Strategic Brand Management Process (p.151–160)

**Step 1: Identify and Develop Brand Plans**
- Define brand positioning and values
- Create a mental map of the brand
- Establish a brand charter

**Step 2: Design and Implement Brand Marketing Programs**
- Choose brand elements
- Integrate marketing activities
- Leverage secondary associations

**Step 3: Measure and Interpret Brand Performance**
- Conduct brand audits
- Design brand tracking studies
- Establish a brand equity management system

**Step 4: Grow and Sustain Brand Equity**
- Define brand architecture
- Manage brand extensions
- Manage brands over time and geographies

### PROCESS 2: Brand Audit

**Definition** (p.965–966):
> "A brand audit is a comprehensive examination of a brand to discover its sources of brand equity."

Unlike a marketing audit (which is internally focused on operations), a brand audit is externally, consumer-focused. It asks:
- From the firm's perspective: what products/services are offered and how are they branded?
- From the consumer's perspective: what deeply held perceptions and beliefs create the true meaning of the brand?

**When to conduct**: During annual planning cycle; when important shifts in strategic direction are likely.

**Two Steps of the Brand Audit**:

**Step 1: Brand Inventory**
Purpose: Provide a current, comprehensive profile of how all products and services are marketed and branded.

Catalogue for each product or service:
- Names, logos, symbols, characters, packaging, slogans, and other trademarks
- Inherent product attributes or characteristics
- Pricing, communications, and distribution policies
- All relevant marketing activity

Digital brand asset inventory additions:
- All digital assets: images, audio, video deployed online
- YouTube videos, white papers, blog posts, sponsored content
- Social media channel presence and consistency

Four insights from digital inventory:
1. Outdated brand accounts that need to be closed or updated
2. Overlapping assets that can be merged or deleted
3. Inaccurate or out-of-date information in existing accounts
4. Digital channels where the brand has no presence

Outcome: Accurate, comprehensive profile of all brand elements, their application, and the supporting marketing program. Also profile competitive brands to determine POPs and PODs.

**Step 2: Brand Exploratory**
Purpose: Understand what consumers actually think and feel about the brand — the consumer's perspective on sources of brand equity.

Preliminary activities:
- Dig through company archives for prior research reports
- Interview internal personnel to understand their beliefs about consumer perceptions
- Diversity of internal opinion reveals inconsistencies and misconceptions

Then conduct qualitative research (free association, projective techniques, ZMET, ethnographic methods) followed by quantitative research (brand awareness, brand image, brand responses, brand relationships).

**Classification of qualitative research** (Levy's three criteria):
- Direction: is the stimulus related to the person or the brand?
- Depth: are responses superficial/concrete or deeper/abstract?
- Diversity: does the technique allow for varied responses?

### FRAMEWORK 2: Brand Architecture Strategy

**Definition** (p.1252):
Brand architecture strategy helps marketers determine which products and services to introduce, and which brand names, logos, symbols to apply.

**Two roles of brand architecture**:
1. **Clarify brand awareness** — improve consumer understanding and communicate similarity and differences between individual products and services
2. **Improve brand image** — maximize transfer of equity between the brand and individual products and services

**Three-Step Process for Developing Brand Architecture** (p.1253):

**Step 1: Defining Brand Potential**
Three components:
- **Brand vision** — management's view of the brand's long-term potential; aspirational but achievable; relates to higher-order purpose
- **Brand boundaries** — which products/services the brand should offer, which benefits it should supply, which needs it should satisfy; the "Spandex Rule" — just because you can stretch it doesn't mean you should
- **Brand positioning** — where the brand stands relative to competitors in consumers' minds

**Step 2: Identifying Brand Extension Opportunities**
Select product and service extensions that allow the brand to achieve its potential while maintaining the core brand positioning (see Ch. 13 — brand extensions).

**Step 3: Branding New Products and Services**
Decide on brand elements and positioning for each new product.

**Brand-Product Matrix** (p.1254):
- Rows = brand-product relationships (brand extension strategy)
- Columns = product-brand relationships (brand portfolio strategy)
- Breadth = how many different categories covered by a brand
- Depth = how many variants exist in each category

**Key terms**:
- Brand line: all products sold under a particular brand
- Brand portfolio: set of all brands a firm offers in a particular category
- Brand mix: set of all brand lines a firm offers

**Two Main Architecture Strategies**:
1. **Branded House** (BH) — corporate name dominates (e.g., Google products, Virgin Group)
2. **House of Brands** (HOB) — individual product brands dominate; corporate brand is invisible to consumers (e.g., Procter & Gamble with Tide, Pampers, Pantene)

**Brand Hierarchy**:
Multiple levels of brand elements may be applied:
- Corporate brand (highest level)
- Family brand
- Individual brand
- Modifier (item or model)

Rule: Associations at the highest levels should be relevant to as many products as possible at the next lower level. Brands should be well-differentiated at any one level.

**Brand Portfolio Principles**:
- Maximize market coverage while minimizing overlap among brands
- Each brand should have a distinct positioning and target segment
- Identify roles for each brand in the portfolio: flanker brands, cash cow brands, low-end entry-level brands, high-end prestige brands

### FRAMEWORK 3: Brand Extensions

**Advantages of Brand Extensions** (p.1396):
- Facilitate new product acceptance (reduced risk for consumers and retailers)
- Reduce introductory marketing costs (existing awareness and image)
- Provide feedback benefits to the parent brand (can strengthen associations)

**Disadvantages of Brand Extensions**:
- Can confuse or frustrate consumers
- Can encounter retailer resistance (shelf space)
- Can fail and hurt parent brand image
- Can succeed but cannibalize parent brand sales
- Can dilute brand meaning
- Can foreclose the chance to develop a new brand

**Evaluating a Brand Extension** — Five-Step Process (p.1441–1465):
1. Define actual and desired consumer knowledge about the brand
2. Identify possible extension candidates
3. Evaluate the potential of the extension candidate (fit, leverage, consumer acceptance)
4. Design marketing programs to launch extension
5. Evaluate extension success and effects on parent brand equity

**Extension Fit**: The greater the perceived similarity between the extension and the parent brand, the more likely consumers will accept the extension. Two dimensions:
- Category similarity
- Brand concept consistency

**Vertical Brand Extensions**: Extending up (premium) or down (value). Downward stretches risk diluting the brand. Upward stretches risk not being credible.

**Academic Research Guidelines**:
1. Concrete attribute associations are harder to extend than abstract benefit associations
2. The fit of an extension depends on both the product category fit and the brand concept fit
3. Successful extensions can enhance the parent brand's equity; unsuccessful ones can harm it
4. Sub-branding (modifier added to parent brand) can help create perceived distance

---

### PROCESS 4: Brand Revitalization

**When to revitalize**: When brand equity has eroded — awareness has declined, brand image has become negative, brand is no longer relevant.

**Two Basic Approaches** (p.1551):
1. **Expand brand awareness** — enter new markets, reach new customers, identify new uses
2. **Improve brand image** — update associations; fix weak, negative, or irrelevant associations

**Specific Tactics**:
- Expanding brand awareness: find new distribution channels, identify overlooked usage situations, reach new market segments
- Improving brand image: strengthen existing positive associations, add new associations, neutralize negative ones
- Identifying new and completely different ways to use the brand
- Communicating new information about the product

### TOOL 2: Brand Report Card (p.1730–1733)

Rate your brand 1–10 on each characteristic. Compare to major competitors. Approach from a consumer's perspective, not management's.

**10 Characteristics of Strong Brands**:
1. **Managers understand what the brand means to consumers** — research-driven mental maps, brand mantra defined, consumer-driven boundaries for extensions
2. **Brand is properly positioned** — category/competitive/correlational POPs established; desirable/deliverable/differentiated PODs established
3. **Customers receive superior delivery of the benefits they value most** — unmet needs uncovered; relentless focus on maximizing customer experiences
4. **Brand takes advantage of the full repertoire of branding activities** — brand name, logo, symbol, slogan, packaging, signage, URL strategically chosen; integrated push and pull strategies
5. **Marketing and communications are seamlessly integrated** — communicates with one voice; all communications have common meaning; digital and social media leveraged
6. **Brand pricing strategy is based on consumer perceptions of value** — value perceptions estimated; price/cost/quality optimized
7. **Brand uses appropriate imagery to support its personality** — credibility established (expert, trustworthy, likable); user and usage imagery appropriate; brand personality crafted
8. **Brand is innovative and relevant** — product and marketing improvements invested in; stays in touch with customers
9. **Brand architecture is strategically sound** — hierarchy has right associations at each level; portfolio maximizes coverage and minimizes overlap
10. **Company has a system in place to monitor brand equity** — brand charter defines meaning; periodic brand audits conducted; routine tracking studies run; brand equity reports distributed; people assigned responsibility for brand equity

**Seven Deadly Sins of Brand Management** (p.1734–1736):
1. Failure to fully understand the meaning of the brand — managers convince themselves of what consumers know rather than researching it
2. Failure to live up to the brand promise — overpromising and underdelivering
3. Failure to adequately support the brand — expecting brand to remain strong without continued investment
4. Failure to be patient with the brand — taking shortcuts that bypass foundational brand-building
5. Failure to adequately control the brand — haphazard decisions without understanding brand equity impact
6. Failure to properly balance consistency and change — being left behind or becoming a moving target
7. Failure to understand the complexity of brand equity — oversimplifying; trying to equate success with one action

### FRAMEWORK 4: Brand Value Chain (p.359–383)

A structured means to trace the value creation process to understand the financial impact of marketing.

**Value Stages**:
Stage 1: **Marketing Program Investment** — any marketing activity that potentially affects brand equity (product, communication, distribution, pricing)

Stage 2: **Customer Mind-Set** — five dimensions changed by marketing:
- Awareness
- Associations
- Attitudes
- Attachment
- Activity (purchase and engagement)

Stage 3: **Market Performance** — six outcomes of the customer mind-set:
- Price premiums
- Price elasticities
- Market share
- Brand expansion
- Cost structure (reduced marketing costs due to existing equity)
- Brand profitability

Stage 4: **Shareholder Value** — how market performance translates to stock price, P/E ratios, and risk profile

**Multipliers** (filters between stages):
- Between Stage 1 and 2: Program Quality Multiplier (clarity, relevance, distinctiveness, consistency)
- Between Stage 2 and 3: Marketplace Conditions Multiplier (competitive reactions, channel support, customer size/profile)
- Between Stage 3 and 4: Investor Sentiment Multiplier (market dynamics, growth potential, risk profile)

### FRAMEWORK 5: Managing Brands Over Time

**Reinforcing Brands** (p.1516–1546):
- Maintaining brand consistency — the core elements of brand meaning should not change
- Protecting sources of brand equity — ensure the key associations remain strong, favorable, and unique
- Fine-tuning the supporting marketing program — incremental changes acceptable

**Three Critical Questions for Any Proposed Change**:
1. What does the brand currently mean to consumers?
2. What do we want it to mean?
3. Could we convince consumers to move from where they are to where we want to be?

**Key Principle** (p.1524):
> "Consistency should not be equated with rigidity. The key is maintaining the core brand meaning while being willing to update or adjust the supporting marketing program as needed."

**Fortifying vs. Leveraging**:
- Fortifying: investing marketing resources to strengthen existing associations
- Leveraging: using existing equity to build in new directions (extensions, co-branding)
- Risk: excessive leveraging without sufficient fortifying depletes equity

### FRAMEWORK 6: Global Branding

**Ten Commandments to Building Global Customer-Based Brand Equity** (Ch. 15):
1. Understand similarities and differences in the global branding landscape
2. Don't take shortcuts in brand building
3. Establish marketing infrastructure
4. Embrace integrated marketing communications
5. Cultivate brand partnerships
6. Balance standardization and customization
7. Balance global and local control
8. Establish operable guidelines
9. Implement a global brand equity measurement system
10. Leverage brand elements

## BOOK 2: THE 22 IMMUTABLE LAWS OF BRANDING — Al Ries & Laura Ries

### Introduction: Core Philosophy

> "A branding program should be designed to differentiate your product from all the other cattle on the range. Even if all the other cattle on the range look pretty much alike."

> "Successful branding programs are based on the concept of singularity. The objective is to create in the mind of the prospect the perception that there is no other product on the market quite like your product."

> "Marketing is not selling. Marketing is building a brand in the mind of the prospect."

> "Marketing is brand building. The two concepts are so inextricably linked that it's impossible to separate them."

> "That old expression 'Nothing happens until somebody sells something' is being replaced by today's slogan: 'Nothing happens until somebody brands something.'"

> "A brand name is nothing but a word in the mind, albeit a special kind of word."

---

### THE 22 LAWS

#### Law 1: The Law of Expansion
**"The power of a brand is inversely proportional to its scope."**

When you put your brand name on everything, that name loses its power. Line extension, megabranding, variable pricing, and sophisticated marketing techniques are being used to milk brands rather than build them.

Evidence: Chevrolet (10 models) loses to Ford (8 models). AmEx went from 27% market share to 18% by adding too many card types. Levi's jeans market share fell from 31% to 19% after expanding to 27 cuts. Crest went from 36% to 25% market share after expanding from 38 to 50+ SKUs.

> "Short term versus long term. Do you broaden the line in order to increase sales in the short term? Or do you keep a narrow line in order to build the brand in the mind and increase sales in the future?"

**Application for web agencies**: Do not expand your service offering beyond what you can do excellently. Narrow scope = stronger brand position.

---

#### Law 2: The Law of Contraction
**"A brand becomes stronger when you narrow its focus."**

Five-step pattern of retail category killers (Toys R Us, Home Depot, The Gap, Subway, Starbucks):
1. Narrow the focus — a powerful brand always starts by contracting, not expanding
2. Stock in depth — become the destination with breadth within one category
3. Buy cheap — make money on purchasing power, not just margins
4. Sell cheap — pricing advantage from volume buying
5. Dominate the category — ultimate objective of any branding program

> "Good things happen when you contract your brand rather than expand it."

Domino's narrowed to just delivery. Starbucks narrowed to coffee. Subway narrowed to submarines. All became category dominators.

**Application**: Pick one thing your agency does better than anyone in your market. Own that.

---

#### Law 3: The Law of Publicity
**"The birth of a brand is achieved with publicity, not advertising."**

> "Today brands are born, not made. A new brand must be capable of generating favorable publicity in the media or it won't have a chance in the marketplace."

The best way to generate publicity is by being first. Being first in a new category generates enormous publicity:
- Band-Aid: first adhesive bandage
- CNN: first cable news network
- Starbucks: first upscale coffeehouse chain
- Amazon: first online bookstore

> "There's a strong relationship between the two. The news media wants to talk about what's new, what's first, and what's hot, not what's better."

> "What others say about your brand is much more powerful than what you say about it yourself."

> "Strategy should be developed first from a publicity point of view."

**Application**: Build your agency's brand by doing something genuinely first in your market. Create newsworthy content, case studies, or results that earn media attention before paying for ads.

---

#### Law 4: The Law of Advertising
**"Once born, a brand needs advertising to stay healthy."**

> "Your advertising budget is like a country's defense budget. Those massive advertising dollars don't buy you anything; they just keep you from losing market share to your competition."

Two phases of brand communication:
1. Publicity phase — introduction of a new category; birth of a brand
2. Advertising phase — once the brand is established, advertising maintains position

After the launch publicity dies down, advertising becomes the primary investment tool for brand maintenance.

---

#### Law 5: The Law of the Word
**"A brand should strive to own a word in the mind of the consumer."**

The most powerful concept in branding is owning a word in the prospect's mind. Not a complicated word. Not an invented one. A simple word from the everyday vocabulary.

Examples of word ownership:
- Volvo = safety
- BMW = driving
- Mercedes = prestige
- FedEx = overnight
- Crest = cavities
- Heinz = ketchup
- Kleenex = tissue

> "The most powerful concept in branding is owning a word in the prospect's mind."

Once a brand owns a word, even competitors cannot use it effectively in their own brand positioning.

**Application for brand strategy clients**: Ask clients: what single word should your brand own in your customers' minds? Build all messaging around claiming that word.

---

#### Law 6: The Law of Credentials
**"The crucial ingredient in the success of any brand is its claim to authenticity."**

The best credential is being the category leader — being number one gives you authenticity and authority. Consumers assume the leader is best.

> "The leader doesn't advertise its leadership per se. The leader advertises its essence."

If you cannot be number one in your current category, create a new category in which you can be number one.

---

#### Law 7: The Law of Quality
**"Quality is important, but brands are not built by quality alone."**

Quality is assumed. It is a threshold requirement — you must have it to compete — but it does not build brands by itself. Brands are built by perception, not by production.

> "Quality is a result of delivering quality, but the brand is built in the mind of the consumer."

Two brands with identical quality can have dramatically different brand equity, entirely because of how they are positioned and communicated.

---

#### Law 8: The Law of the Category
**"A leading brand should promote the category, not the brand."**

> "What category does your brand stand for? In the long run, every brand has to stand for a category or it can't survive."

When you're the leader, promoting the category builds the whole market, and since you dominate, you benefit most. Intel does not advertise microprocessors; it advertises "Intel Inside."

Creating a new category is often more powerful than fighting for position in an existing one. Domino's didn't compete with Pizza Hut in sit-down pizza; it created the pizza delivery category.

---

#### Law 9: The Law of the Name
**"In the long run, a brand is nothing more than a name."**

> "The most important branding decision you will ever make is what to name the product or service."

Over time, all product differences become erased. What remains is the brand name. The name is the hook that hangs the brand in the mind.

Good brand name criteria:
- Short
- Simple
- Unique
- Easy to pronounce
- Easy to spell
- Different from competition
- No negative connotations in any language you operate in

---

#### Law 10: The Law of Extensions
**"The easiest way to destroy a brand is to put its name on everything."**

> "More than 90 percent of all new products introduced in the U.S. grocery and drug trade are line extensions."

The problem: management measures the success of the extension but never measures the erosion of the core brand.

> "Big brands should have market shares approaching 50 percent, like Coca-Cola, Heinz, Pop-Tarts, Jell-O, and Gerber's. But it's hard to find more than a few such brands. Most big brands have been line-extended to death."

> "Let sleeping brands lie. Before you launch your next line extension, ask yourself what customers of your current brand will think when they see the line extension."

If the market is moving out from under you, stay where you are and **launch a second brand** rather than extending the first.

---

#### Law 11: The Law of Fellowship
**"In order to build the category, a brand should welcome other brands."**

> "The best thing that happened to Coca-Cola was Pepsi-Cola."

Choice stimulates demand. Competition increases the noise level and category awareness, which tends to increase sales for the whole category. Brands that try to monopolize a category often end up weakening it.

Optimal structure: Two major brands per category. Examples: Coke/Pepsi, Listerine/Scope, Kodak/Fuji, Duracell/Energizer. More than two can create confusion and reduce per-capita consumption.

> "Market share is not based on merit, but on the power of the brand in the mind."

---

#### Law 12: The Law of the Generic
**"One of the fastest routes to failure is giving a brand a generic name."**

The mind doesn't deal in letters or words — it deals in sounds. Generic words disappear into the ether no matter how you capitalize them. You cannot trademark a category; you can only own a unique name within it.

Principle: Take a regular word and use it out of context to connote the primary attribute of your brand.
- "Blockbuster" for video rentals (borrowed from Hollywood)
- "Staples" for office supplies (double entendre)
- "Budget" for car rental (suggests low price)
- "Lexus" = "luxury" rearranged

> "Sometimes you can carve out a brand name by cutting a generic in half. Intelligent Chip Company → Intel Corp."

---

#### Law 13: The Law of the Company
**"Brands are brands. Companies are companies. There is a difference."**

> "Brand names should almost always take precedence over company names. Consumers buy brands, they don't buy companies."

When to use the company name as brand name: When the company name is itself a strong brand (GE, IBM, Xerox, Intel, Coca-Cola).

When to subordinate the company name: When the brand name is stronger or more relevant than the company (Tide > Procter & Gamble, Pampers > Procter & Gamble).

When combining company and brand: The brand name is primary; the company name is secondary. Example: General Motors Cadillac (Cadillac is the brand consumers care about).

---

#### Law 14: The Law of Subbrands
**"What branding builds, subbranding can destroy."**

Subbranding is the practice of appending a modifier to a strong brand name (Holiday Inn Select, Holiday Inn Express, Holiday Inn SunSpree).

The problem: Instead of building a new, distinctive brand (which could own a category), you dilute the core brand. The modifier is a generic, and the mind does not register it as a separate brand — it just registers a weakened version of the parent brand.

> "Subbranding is line extension by another name. It always weakens the parent brand."

Better strategy: Build a new, separate brand for each new positioning or segment.

---

#### Law 15: The Law of Siblings
**"There is a time and a place to launch a second brand."**

A sibling strategy — creating a family of individual brands in the same category — can dominate a market over time. Examples:
- P&G: Tide, Cheer, Bold, Gain, Era, Dash, Dreft, Ivory Snow (8 distinct laundry detergents)
- Time Warner: Time, Sports Illustrated, People, Fortune, Money, Entertainment Weekly

**Six Rules for a Sibling Strategy**:
1. Create individual brands, each with its own name, identity, and positioning
2. Each sibling must be a separate, distinct brand — not a variation of the parent
3. Each sibling must be positioned in a different place on the same ladder (same category)
4. Each sibling should have a different, distinctive name — avoid alliteration
5. Launch a new sibling only when you can create a new category
6. Keep control of the sibling family at the highest level to avoid sibling rivalry (brands copying each other)

---

#### Law 16: The Law of Shape
**"A brand's logotype should be designed to fit the eyes. Both eyes."**

The ideal shape for a logotype is **horizontal** — roughly 2.25 units wide by 1 unit high. This fits the binocular vision of a human face and creates maximum impact in all locations.

Vertical logotypes are at a severe disadvantage in the "neon jungle" (storefronts, signage, websites).

Priority order for logotype design:
1. Legibility — the words must be easily read
2. Shape — horizontal is superior
3. Type style — only slightly influences communication vs. legibility

> "The words (Rolex, Ralph Lauren, Rolls-Royce) are what communicate the power of the brands. The typefaces used in their logotypes can help or hinder the communication process, but only slightly."

**Application**: Every brand logo we design should default to a horizontal format unless there is strong strategic reason for an exception. Legibility is non-negotiable.

---

#### Law 17: The Law of Color
**"A brand should use a color that is the opposite of its major competitor's."**

Color is a powerful memory trigger. It can become a brand asset almost as important as the name.

- Kodak = yellow
- Fuji = green
- IBM = blue
- Hertz = yellow
- Avis = red
- UPS = brown

Rule: Choose a color opposite to your major competitor's color. If they are red, choose blue. If they are blue, choose red. This creates clear visual differentiation.

Secondary rule: Consistency. Once you choose a color, use it everywhere, always.

**Application**: In brand style guides, color selection should begin with competitive color analysis. Own the color that competitors don't.

---

#### Law 18: The Law of Borders
**"There are no barriers to global branding. A brand should know no borders."**

Successful brands expand globally while maintaining their core positioning. The same branding rules apply worldwide. The brand that becomes first in the mind globally is the brand that wins globally.

**Application**: Build websites and brand systems with international expansion in mind.

---

#### Law 19: The Law of Consistency
**"A brand is not built overnight. Success is measured in decades, not years."**

> "Change a brand's positioning and you destroy the brand. The brand may survive, but only a shadow of its former self."

Brands that consistently stand for the same thing for decades build incredible equity. Volvo has stood for safety for 30+ years. FedEx has meant overnight delivery for 30+ years.

The brands that fail are those that keep repositioning — trying to chase the market, follow trends, or respond to competitive pressure by changing their core identity.

**Application**: Once a brand positioning is established, protect it fiercely. Resist the temptation to pivot. Build on it instead.

---

#### Law 20: The Law of Change
**"Brands can be changed, but only infrequently and only very carefully."**

Three situations where changing a brand is feasible:
1. **The brand is weak or nonexistent in the mind** — no established identity, so you can do anything
2. **You want to move the brand down the food chain** — permanently lowering price requires repositioning
3. **The brand is in a slow-moving category** — can gradually reposition without consumer awareness

Brand changing does not occur inside a company. It occurs inside the mind of the consumer. Keep sights on the target: the consumer's mind.

---

#### Law 21: The Law of Mortality
**"No brand will live forever. Euthanasia is often the best solution."**

Brands can outlive their usefulness. When a brand is so tied to a specific era, product form, or consumer segment that is disappearing, it may be better to let the brand die gracefully rather than spend resources trying to revitalize it.

> "Company leadership can sometimes be blinded by the history and success of a brand to the extent that they can't see when the brand has had its day."

---

#### Law 22: The Law of Singularity
**"The most important aspect of a brand is its single-mindedness."**

A brand must stand for one thing, and only one thing. Not multiple things for multiple people.

> "A brand is a singular idea or concept that you own inside the mind of the prospect."

When consumers cannot easily answer the question "What does this brand stand for?", the brand has lost its singularity and therefore its power.

**Application**: Every brand strategy deliverable should answer: what is the single word/idea this brand owns?

---

### THE 11 IMMUTABLE LAWS OF INTERNET BRANDING (Bonus Section)

#### Internet Law 1: The Law of Either/Or
An Internet brand can be either a business brand or an Internet brand, but not both. A physical-world brand that goes online needs a separate, distinct Internet brand identity. Offline giants (Barnes & Noble.com, Walmart.com, Sears.com) that simply extended their offline brands online failed. New, pure-play Internet brands won by owning the online space independently.

**Application**: Do not name a digital product/service arm after the parent company if it serves a different audience or model.

#### Internet Law 2: The Law of Interactivity
Without interactivity, a website is not a website. It is just a brochure. Interactivity — the ability for users to do something — is what creates value and engagement.

#### Internet Law 3: The Law of the Common Name
The biggest mistake you can make on the Internet is using a common or generic name for a brand. Pets.com, Garden.com, eToys.com, Hardware.com — all failed in part because generic names cannot build brand identity.

#### Internet Law 4: The Law of the Proper Name
Your name is your brand on the Internet. More so than anywhere else, because discovery on the Internet depends on knowing the name. You must be able to say it, spell it, and remember it.

Good Internet names: Yahoo!, Google, Amazon, eBay.
The best Internet names are short, unique, easy to spell, and memorable.

#### Internet Law 5: The Law of Singularity
The same principle applies online as offline: the most powerful Internet brands have a singular focus. Amazon dominated books before expanding. eBay dominated auctions.

#### Internet Law 6: The Law of Internet Advertising
Internet advertising cannot build Internet brands. Only word-of-mouth, PR, and viral marketing can launch an Internet brand. Once launched, traditional advertising can support and maintain it.

#### Internet Law 7: The Law of Globalism
The Internet is inherently global. A brand built on the Internet has global reach from day one. This is an enormous advantage for new brands — geographic borders become irrelevant.

#### Internet Law 8: The Law of Time
Being first is not enough. You must be first in the mind, and you must be there when the mind is ready to receive you. Timing matters.

#### Internet Law 9: The Law of Vanity
The biggest mistake in Internet branding is putting everyone on the Internet. Vanity — the belief that every product/service needs an Internet brand — leads to fragmentation and wasted investment.

#### Internet Law 10: The Law of Divergence
The Internet will not converge all media into one. It is a separate medium with its own characteristics, its own brands, and its own laws.

#### Internet Law 11: The Law of Transformation
The Internet changes industries. Brands built for the pre-Internet era may need transformation — new names, new positioning, new approaches — to survive in an Internet-dominated market.

## PRACTICAL TOOLS AND WORKSHEETS

### Tool 1: Positioning Statement Template (from Keller)

"For [target market], [brand name] is the [frame of reference/category] that [point-of-difference] because [reason to believe]."

Example: "For young, active soft-drink consumers who have little time for sleep, Mountain Dew is the soft drink that gives you more energy than any other brand because it has a higher level of caffeine."

---

### Tool 2: Brand Mantra Development Process (from Keller)

Step 1: Conduct brand audit to understand current brand knowledge structures
Step 2: Identify core brand associations (top 3–5 things the brand stands for)
Step 3: Brainstorm brand mantra candidates using the three-component structure:
  - Brand Function (what the brand does)
  - Descriptive Modifier (what type of function)
  - Emotional Modifier (how it makes people feel)
Step 4: Test candidates against criteria: communicate, simplify, inspire
Step 5: Implement — distribute to all employees and partners; use as screening device

---

### Tool 3: Brand Audit Checklist (from Keller Ch. 9)

**Brand Inventory Checklist**:
- [ ] All brand names in use across all products and services
- [ ] All logos and their variations
- [ ] All slogans, taglines, and messaging
- [ ] All packaging designs
- [ ] All digital assets (website, social profiles, videos, images)
- [ ] All communications materials (ads, brochures, proposals)
- [ ] Pricing policy across segments and channels
- [ ] Distribution channels and how the brand appears in each
- [ ] Competitive brand profiles (for POPs and PODs)

**Brand Exploratory Questions**:
- What is the first thing you think of when you hear [brand name]?
- What are the strongest associations you have with this brand?
- What images or feelings does the brand evoke?
- Who do you picture using this brand?
- What three words would you use to describe this brand?
- How does this brand differ from [competitor]?
- What would you miss most if this brand disappeared?

---

### Tool 4: The 5 Laws for Naming a Brand (synthesised from Ries & Keller)

1. **Short** — one or two syllables preferred
2. **Distinctive** — not a generic word used in its normal context
3. **Easy** — simple to pronounce, spell, and remember
4. **Unique** — ownable, not easily confused with competitors
5. **Category-appropriate** — evokes or creates associations relevant to what the brand does

---

### Tool 5: Brand Color Selection Framework (from Ries)

1. Survey the color landscape of your competitive category
2. Identify the primary color used by the market leader
3. Choose a distinctly different color — opposite on the color wheel if possible
4. Lock this color across all brand touchpoints: website, logo, marketing materials, signage
5. Do not change the color once established — consistency builds recognition over time

---

### Tool 6: Brand Report Card (Keller — 10-Point Scale)

Score your brand 1–10 (1 = extremely poor, 10 = extremely good):

| # | Characteristic | Score |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Managers understand what the brand means to consumers | |
| 2 | The brand is properly positioned (POPs and PODs) | |
| 3 | Customers receive superior delivery of the benefits they value most | |
| 4 | Brand takes advantage of full repertoire of branding activities | |
| 5 | Marketing and communications are seamlessly integrated | |
| 6 | Pricing strategy is based on consumer perceptions of value | |
| 7 | Brand uses appropriate imagery to support its personality | |
| 8 | Brand is innovative and relevant | |
| 9 | Brand architecture is strategically sound (for multi-brand companies) | |
| 10 | Company has a system in place to monitor brand equity | |

## APPLICATIONS FOR WEB AGENCY WORK

### Brand Strategy Deliverables (what to produce for a client)

Based on the frameworks above, a complete brand strategy deliverable for a web agency client should include:

1. **Brand Audit** (Keller Ch. 9)
   - Brand inventory: all existing touchpoints, assets, and messaging
   - Brand exploratory: consumer research (or client-facing equivalent)
   - Competitive analysis: POPs and PODs identified

2. **Brand Positioning** (Keller Ch. 2)
   - Target market definition
   - Frame of reference (category)
   - Points-of-parity (table stakes to compete)
   - Points-of-difference (competitive advantage)
   - Formal positioning statement

3. **Brand Mantra** (Keller Ch. 2)
   - 3–5 word internal compass
   - Function + Descriptive Modifier + Emotional Modifier

4. **Brand Singularity** (Ries)
   - The single word the brand should own in the customer's mind
   - All messaging evaluated against this word

5. **Brand Elements** (Keller Ch. 4)
   - Name evaluation against 6 criteria
   - Logo/symbol design rationale
   - Color selection (competitive differentiation)
   - Tagline/slogan
   - URL and digital presence

6. **Brand Architecture** (Keller Ch. 12)
   - Branded house vs. house of brands decision
   - Product/brand matrix
   - Hierarchy rules

### Brand Style Guide Content (what belongs in a brand style guide)

A web agency brand style guide should document:

- Brand positioning statement
- Brand mantra
- Target audience profile
- Voice and tone guidelines
- Logo usage rules (including shape — prefer horizontal, p.78 Ries)
- Color palette with rationale (based on competitive landscape, Law 17 Ries)
- Typography
- Imagery direction (user profiles, usage scenarios, Law 7 in Ries)
- Messaging hierarchy (primary claim → supporting claims → RTBs)
- POPs and PODs for use in copy direction
- Examples of on-brand and off-brand usage

### Brand Storytelling Principles (synthesised from both books)

1. Brand stories must be grounded in what the brand genuinely stands for (brand mantra)
2. Stories should communicate the brand's unique word ownership (Law of the Word)
3. Credibility comes from being first, not from claiming to be best (Law of Credentials)
4. Stories are more powerful from third-party sources than from the brand itself (Law of Publicity)
5. Brand imagery must consistently reinforce the brand's positioning, personality, and target audience associations (CBBE pyramid — Imagery building block)
6. Every touchpoint tells part of the brand story — including logo shape, color, name, and packaging (Brand Elements framework)

### Brand Positioning for Specific Industries

Using Keller's POPs/PODs framework:
- **POPs** are category membership signals — what the brand must have to be credible in its category
- **PODs** are competitive advantage claims — what makes the brand the better choice
- The best PODs are benefits (what the customer gets), not features (what the product does)
- The most durable PODs are intangible/emotional (harder to copy than functional attributes)
