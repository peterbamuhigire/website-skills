# Visitor Psychology and Cultural Fit Review

Parent skill: [cro-audit](../SKILL.md). Read when a page already passes the heuristic checklist but still needs a deeper review of how it will feel to a specific visitor: which psychological levers it uses, whether it suits the audience's culture and temperament, and whether any lever has tipped into pressure.

Use this file alongside `heuristic-checklist.md` (Sections 4, 7 and 10 cover social proof, trust and Cialdini's six principles) and `ethical-persuasion-gate.md` (what is forbidden). This file adds the levers and audience-fit checks those two files do not teach. Every lever here must still pass the ethical gate.

---

## 1. Frame the review with three questions

Before scoring any lever, answer three framing questions for the page. A "no" on any of them outranks every finding below.

| Question | What a pass looks like |
|---|---|
| Is the page specific? | It is written for a named audience segment: role, problem, culture, register. Nothing is addressed to "everyone". |
| Is the page credible? | Authority, consistency and transparency are visible within the first screen and survive a closer look. |
| Is the page attractive? | It is pleasant to look at, emotionally engaging and rewarding to use: the visitor gains something from each interaction. |

---

## 2. Lever-by-lever review

For each lever, record: present / absent / overdone, the evidence on the page, and the change you recommend.

### Reciprocity: shape of the gift

- The gift is useful on its own, not a teaser that only works after purchase.
- It feels voluntary (no strings visible at the moment of giving), fitted to the visitor's situation, and, where possible, unexpected.
- The effort behind it may be stated plainly ("a 40-page field guide built from our project files") but never inflated.
- Ungated value (articles, tools, calculators) counts. Do not gate everything.

### Commitment and consistency: honest small steps

- Sequence small, easy agreements before the main ask (a preference question, a free assessment, a trial).
- Each step must be reversible and must not trap the visitor. Pre-ticked consent and opt-out defaults are rejected; they buy one conversion and lose the relationship.

### Social proof: choose the strongest honest form

Rank the proof available, then lead with the strongest that is true:

1. Endorsement from a recognised expert or professional body in the visitor's field.
2. A known public figure with a genuine connection to the offer.
3. Volume ("used by N firms"), only with a sourced, current number.
4. People the visitor knows or resembles (peer reviews, named local clients).

Rules:

- Match proof to the visitor: same role, same problem, same context beats a bigger but distant name.
- Never state that most people fail to do the desired thing; that normalises the undesired behaviour. Say what the successful group does.
- Live-activity indicators ("people booking today") are permitted only when fed by real data. Otherwise they are forbidden by the ethical gate.
- Customer-created content (reviews, photos, stories) usually reads as less biased than brand copy; collect it with consent and date it.

### Liking

Check for each trigger and note how the page earns it:

- Similarity: photography of people who resemble the target customer; copy in their own words.
- Familiarity: consistent visual identity across every touchpoint, so repeated exposure builds recognition.
- Shared values stated plainly, with evidence of acting on them.
- Warmth without flattery; praise of the visitor must be genuine or absent.
- Association with things the audience already values (causes, places, institutions), only where the link is real.

### Authority

- Credentials are specific (named qualification, body, year), never generic ("industry-leading").
- Place credentials next to the claim they support, not only on the About page.
- Media and accreditation marks must be authorised and current.

### Scarcity and urgency

- Identify the type: quantity (limited stock or capacity) or time (a real deadline).
- Use it only when it is true and will still be true if the visitor returns tomorrow.
- Combining genuine scarcity with genuine social proof is legitimate; combining invented versions of either is not.
- Overuse triggers reactance (section 5).

## 3. Language levers

| Lever | Rule | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Concrete wording | Replace abstractions with observable, measurable outcomes the business can support. | Could the visitor picture it or measure it? |
| Sensory detail | Where the offer is experienced physically (food, hospitality, spaces, products), describe texture, sound, light, taste. | Does at least one passage let the reader feel the experience? |
| Story | Use a short customer or founder story to carry a point that facts alone leave flat. Keep facts nearby for the analytical reader. | Is there a named person, a problem, a turn and an outcome? |
| Loss or gain frame | Frame by what the visitor avoids losing when the purchase protects against risk (insurance, security, compliance, maintenance). Frame by what they gain when the purchase is aspirational or for pleasure (travel, design, education, leisure). | Does the frame match the product type? Is a loss frame free of fear-mongering? |
| Positive framing of the same fact | Present verified figures in their positive form ("nine in ten projects finish on schedule" rather than "one in ten runs late") while keeping them accurate and sourced. | Is the figure real, dated and not cherry-picked? |
| Reason given | Attach a genuine reason to every request ("Book this week because the survey team is in your district on Thursday"). A reason makes compliance easier; a false reason is deception. | Does each CTA or request carry a true "because"? |
| Direct address | Write in the second person; the visitor should feel addressed, not described. | Count "you/your" against "we/our" in the hero and first section; the visitor should dominate. |
| Action words | Words such as "free", "new", "now", "instantly", "proven", "guaranteed" are only acceptable when literally true on this page. | Is every such word backed by a fact or policy? |
| Reading level | Match the audience's reading level: neither talking down nor over their heads. Short sentences, bullets for complex detail, one bold phrase per section for scanners. | Does a scan of headings and bold text carry the argument? |

## 4. Cognitive and social effects to check

| Effect | What to look for | Recommended action |
|---|---|---|
| Priming | Imagery, colour and opening words set the mood before the offer appears. | Make the first screen prime the emotion the offer resolves (calm for finance, appetite for food); remove imagery that primes the wrong state. |
| Anchoring | The first number seen calibrates later value judgements. | Show the fuller or premium option first on pricing tables; never invent a "was" price. |
| Social comparison | Visitors compare themselves with similar others. | Show customers slightly further along than the visitor, so progress looks reachable rather than out of reach. |
| Conformity labels | "Most chosen", "best-seller" tags steer choice. | Use only when the label is true for a stated period. |
| Mere exposure | Repeated, consistent brand exposure raises liking. | Keep identity, tone and imagery consistent across site, email and social. |
| Processing fluency | Easy-to-read text and clean layout are judged more trustworthy. | Remove visual noise, weak contrast and decorative fonts from any claim that must be believed. Route typography decisions to design-system-skills. |
| Ongoing one-sided familiarity | A recurring personal voice (founder video, regular column) builds a felt relationship over time. | Consider a consistent human presenter for recurring content; keep them real and named. |

## 5. Reactance check (pressure audit)

Visitors resist when they feel their freedom is being taken away. Mark any of the following as a failure that outweighs the lever's benefit:

- Countdown timers that reset, or deadlines that are not enforced.
- Pop-ups that interrupt before the visitor has read anything.
- CTA copy that shames the decline option.
- Repeated identical urgency messages on every section.
- Forced registration before basic information (including price range) is visible.

Remedy: remove the pressure device, restore a visible "not now" path, and move the reason for acting into the copy itself.

## 6. Data requests and the privacy gap

People say privacy matters a great deal yet often share data for small rewards; they are, however, very sensitive to signals that data might be misused. Design every data request to remove those signals:

1. State what is collected and why, in plain words, next to the field (not only in the policy).
2. Ask for the minimum now; ask for more only when the visitor has a reason to give it.
3. Write consent and opt-in wording in everyday language; no legal phrasing at the point of capture.
4. Link the privacy policy from the form itself.

Route field-level design to `skills/build/page-builder/references/form-question-wording-and-data-design.md`.

## 7. Engagement mechanics gate

Before adding progress bars, badges, streaks, levels, leaderboards or content released at milestones, confirm that the mechanic serves at least one intrinsic need and genuinely benefits the visitor:

| Need | Mechanic that supports it | Mechanic that undermines it |
|---|---|---|
| Autonomy (control over the experience) | Optional paths, saved preferences, skip options | Compulsory sequences, nagging reminders |
| Mastery (growing competence) | Profile or course completion indicators, skill checklists | Points for trivial actions |
| Relatedness (belonging) | Member groups, peer showcases, community Q&A | Public rankings that shame low participants |

Rule: if the mechanic exists mainly to extract more clicks or data, remove it. Extrinsic rewards bolted onto something people already enjoy can reduce their interest.

## 8. Sound and video rules

- Never autoplay audio. Autoplay video only when muted, short, and pausable, and never on low-bandwidth mobile views.
- If sound is part of the experience (hospitality, events, entertainment), make it opt-in and match tempo and genre to the brand's personality: calm and slow for premium or reflective brands, brighter for youth and retail.
- Prefer video testimonials to text where consent and quality allow; show a real human face in the thumbnail.
- Pair every important spoken point with a visual or text equivalent (captions, transcript) for accessibility and for visitors who read rather than listen.

---

## 9. Identity, community and belonging

- Give visitors a way to signal the values and affiliations they share with the brand (member badges, pledges, public case studies they are proud to appear in).
- Community features (forums, groups, comment spaces, alumni networks) raise loyalty because members invest time and relationships. Only launch one the client can moderate.
- Fear of missing out can drive sharing when access is genuinely exclusive or time-limited; never manufacture it.

## 10. Personality fit

Where research gives a dominant temperament for the audience, tune the page accordingly. Treat this as a hypothesis to test, not a diagnosis.

| Dominant trait in audience | Page emphasis |
|---|---|
| Openness (curious, creative) | More novel visuals, exploratory content, ideas and experiments |
| Conscientiousness (organised, methodical) | Clear structure, specifications, process steps, precise detail |
| Extraversion (sociable, energetic) | Livelier visuals, social proof, community and events |
| Agreeableness (trusting, cooperative) | Warm language, relationship stories, team photographs |
| Neuroticism (anxious, reactive) | Reassurance, guarantees, explicit privacy protection, clear next steps |

---

## 11. Cultural fit review

Culture shapes which levers work. Use national-culture dimensions as a starting hypothesis, then verify with in-market people (see `skills/orchestration/africa-excellence/references/cultural-patterns.md` for East and Central African patterns).

| Dimension | Lower-scoring audiences tend to prefer | Higher-scoring audiences tend to prefer |
|---|---|---|
| Power distance | Informal tone, flat hierarchy, open participation | Formal register, visible credentials and institutional marks, restrained humour |
| Individualism | Group benefit, family and community imagery, belonging | Personal benefit, self-improvement, individual success stories |
| Achievement orientation | Quality of life, care, relationships | Competition, winning, status and success stories |
| Uncertainty avoidance | Brief copy, tolerance of ambiguity | Detailed explanation, full FAQs, explicit guarantees, visible return and refund terms |
| Long-term orientation | Quick results, tradition, proven track record | Patience, future investment, innovation, long relationships |

### Cultural design checklist

- [ ] Colour choices checked against the target market's meanings (route to design-system-skills colour guidance).
- [ ] Language register matches the market's formality norms.
- [ ] Imagery and copy reflect the market's balance of individual and group benefit.
- [ ] Authority signals and tone match the expected power distance.
- [ ] Explanations, guarantees and FAQs are sufficient for the market's tolerance of uncertainty.
- [ ] Localisation goes beyond translation: idioms, humour and formality have been reviewed by a native speaker.

---

## 12. Output

Add a "Visitor psychology and cultural fit" section to the audit report with:

1. The three framing answers (section 1).
2. A lever table: lever, status, evidence, recommendation.
3. Any reactance failures (section 5), listed first.
4. Cultural and personality fit notes with the evidence used and what still needs in-market verification.
5. Test hypotheses for any lever change whose effect is uncertain (record them in the experiment backlog, not as promised lifts).
