## 2. Write about the field, not the product

Most readers search for help with a problem long before they search for a supplier. Topics about the field reach them earlier in the buying journey; topics about the product reach only those already looking for it.

Procedure:

1. Write the service or product in one line (for example "time-management coaching").
2. List the outcomes and problems a reader would search for without knowing the service exists ("how to finish work on time", "why meetings eat the week").
3. Compare, in a keyword tool, the demand for the outcome phrasing against the demand for the service phrasing. Record the figures with the tool and date; do not state volumes in copy unless they have been checked for the target market.
4. Where the outcome phrasing clearly has more demand, brief field-level posts that link to the service page as the next step (see `seo-search/seo/references/content-growth-tactics.md` "Internal Linking: Content Clusters to Money Pages").
5. Keep the business relevant: every field-level idea must still connect to a problem the offer solves. Discard topics that attract readers the business cannot serve.

## 3. Segment multiplier

One sound topic can serve several reader groups when each version genuinely changes for that group.

Procedure:

1. Take an approved topic ("social media marketing").
2. List only the segments the business actually serves or wants (for example clinics, start-ups, job seekers, schools).
3. For each segment, write down what changes: the constraints, vocabulary, regulations, budgets, examples, and the decision the reader faces.
4. Keep a segment variant only if at least three of those elements differ. If only the noun changes, drop the variant; near-identical pages compete with one another and read as templated.
5. Title pattern: "[Topic] for [segment]: [segment-specific outcome or constraint]".

## 4. Event and conference report

Readers who could not attend value a clear account; the post also signals that the business takes part in its industry.

Procedure:

1. Before the event, list the sessions most relevant to the audience and note questions to answer.
2. During it, capture claims, examples, and quotations with the speaker's name and session title.
3. Structure: why the event matters to this reader → three to five main ideas → what they mean for the reader's decisions → what the business is doing differently as a result.
4. Attribute every idea to its speaker; check any figure a speaker gave before repeating it.
5. Tell the speakers and organisers when the post is live; they often share it.

## 5. Takeaways from a published work or research report

A condensed, applied reading of a respected work or report saves the reader time and positions the writer as well read.

Procedure:

1. Choose a work or report the audience has heard of or should know.
2. Title pattern: "[N] Takeaways from [Work] for [Audience]".
3. For each takeaway, paraphrase the idea in your own words, then add how it applies to the reader's market, with one local or sector example.
4. Cite author, title, year, and publisher. Keep direct quotations short and rare; never reproduce chapters, tables, or long passages.
5. Add a closing section on where the work does not fit this audience; that judgement is the original contribution.

## 6. Question mining on Q&A sites and forums

Public question sites and forums (for example Quora, Reddit communities, Stack Exchange sites, sector forums) show questions in the audience's own words, often with a visible count of followers, votes, or replies.

Procedure:

1. Search the sites where the audience asks questions, using the category and problem terms from section 2.
2. Record each promising question verbatim, with its link and the visible interest signal (followers, votes, replies, views). Treat these signals as relative, not as search demand.
3. Look first for these question shapes, which map closely to commercial value:
   - "What are the best resources to learn [area]?"
   - "What are the best tools or apps for [task or niche]?"
   - "How can I [outcome the offer delivers]?"
   - "Is [option] worth it for [situation]?"
4. Group near-identical questions; the group becomes one idea whose title echoes the most common wording.
5. Write the full answer as a blog post. If the business also answers on the site, follow the participation rules in `content-copy/blog-writer/references/traffic-channel-menu-and-evaluation.md` section 4.
6. Record the source bucket as "audience questions" in the backlog so the trace in `SKILL.md` stays intact.

## 7. Interview material

Interviews the business gives (to podcasts, press, panels) and interviews it conducts both contain ready-made ideas.

Procedure:

1. Collect recordings or notes of interviews given and received.
2. Transcribe, then mark each passage where a question received a substantive answer.
3. Turn each strong passage into either a question-led post or a condensed Q&A with added context.
4. Obtain the other party's consent before publishing their words; send them the draft for factual checks.
5. Edit for reading: remove filler, add headings, and add links or evidence the spoken answer lacked.

## 8. Opinion and contested questions: guardrails

The method itself lives in Methods 14 and 19 of [ideation-frameworks.md](ideation-frameworks.md). Before approving an opinion idea, confirm that:

- the writer holds a genuine position and can state it in one sentence;
- the post will set out the strongest opposing view fairly;
- factual claims about the news item are checked and dated;
- the topic connects to the audience's decisions, not only to attention.

## 9. Resource lists: notification step

Method 16 in [ideation-frameworks.md](ideation-frameworks.md) covers roundups. Add one step: when the list is published, tell each featured person or organisation, briefly and without asking for anything. Featured parties often share the post. Include a resource only on merit, never in exchange for a share.

## 10. Owned-material mining

Much publishable thinking already exists in material the business has produced for other purposes.

Sources to inspect:

| Owned material | Idea it yields |
|---|---|
| Talks, webinars, workshops | One post per section; audience questions become follow-up posts |
| Videos and course lessons | Transcribe and edit into a how-to or explainer |
| Podcast episodes or audio notes | Transcribe; turn the main argument into an opinion or guide |
| Sales and support emails | Each well-answered question becomes a post (see Methods 5 and 6) |
| Proposals and audit reports (anonymised, with permission) | Case study or "what we check" checklist post |
| A longer planned work (guide, course, title) | Serialise it: one chapter or lesson idea per post, gathering reader feedback before the long version |
| A run of related posts | Combine and expand into a flagship guide (see [idea-sources-and-series.md](idea-sources-and-series.md) "Build series, not isolated posts") |

Rules:

- A transcript is raw material, not a post. Edit for reading, add structure, and add what the spoken version skipped.
- When a series of posts is later combined into a longer work, expand it with new context, stories, and examples; readers expect more than the collected posts.
- Record each idea's source item in the backlog so reviewers can trace it.
