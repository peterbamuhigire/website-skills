# Advanced Business English

Parent skill: [language-standards](../SKILL.md). Read when making professional English decisions: register, argument phrasing, plain-language rewriting, apologies, report structure, contract-register verbs and common grammar errors. Word-level formal/informal pairs live in `content-writing/references/business-vocabulary.md`.

## 1. Register Decisions

- Formal: recommendation letters, legal documents, official reports, senior emails, formal presentations.
- Informal: peer conversation, team chat, networking.
- Rule: match register to the relationship and the document. Do not over-formalise by default; clear and direct reads as more authoritative than complex and obscure. Reserve third person ("the Company", "the Client") for legal documents; use "we" and "you" elsewhere.

## 2. Plain Language: the Anti-Jargon Method

Jargon and stock phrases signal vagueness. Clear writing shows confidence and respects the reader, who is a person whatever their seniority.

Rewrite procedure:
1. Cut every word that adds no meaning.
2. Replace abstractions with named outcomes (profit, customers, delivery time).
3. Switch third person to "we" and "you".
4. Prefer active voice ("Gary launched the project").
5. Keep sentences short and direct.

Illustrative before and after (original):
- Before: "Our dedicated professionals deliver holistic, value-added solutions that leverage synergies across the client lifecycle."
- After: "We help Kampala retailers sell more and lose less stock. We start by counting what you have."

Checklist: simple sentences; jargon removed; personal pronouns; specific outcomes; active voice; one idea per sentence.

## 3. Argument and Discussion Phrases

| Function | Phrases |
|---|---|
| State a view | I would argue that; It seems to me that; I tend to think that; In my experience |
| Report others' views | Some people think that; Many feel that; It is often suggested that |
| Refute | I am unconvinced that; It is hard to accept that; There is little evidence to support that |
| Support | For instance; Indeed; Statistics demonstrate; Let me illustrate |
| Define | In other words; By this I mean; To be more precise |
| Propose | One approach would be; A second possibility is; The crucial point is |
| Concede then counter | While I admit that ..., I would argue that ...; Admittedly ... arguably ... |
| Conclude | In conclusion; On balance; Ultimately; To sum up |

### Raising and answering a weak argument
1. Introduce it: "It could be argued that ..."
2. Grant its limited truth: "To a limited extent this is true."
3. Dismantle it: "However, the implication that ... oversimplifies the position." or "It is therefore wrong to suggest that ..."

### Transition frequency
- Use sparingly (they pad): Furthermore, Moreover, Additionally, Nevertheless, Consequently, In contrast.
- Use moderately: While, Although, Despite the fact that, However, Thus, Meanwhile.

## 4. Listener-Centred Communication

- Answer "what is in it for them?" in the first sentence of any persuasive message.
- Start with the reader's difficulty, then show relief. Do not list features only; describe the removal of the pain.
- Illustrative slot: "No more queuing at the mobile-money agent to pay each supplier. One payment run each Friday."
- Details separate lively from generic: "Nile perch", not "fish".
- Barriers to clear communication: empty superlatives (if everything is "incredible", nothing is); defensiveness about being wrong; approval-seeking that reads as insincere; silence in an emotional moment.

## 5. Apologising

1. Acknowledge the mistake plainly; do not argue or hide it.
2. State what happened, factually, without excess self-blame.
3. Take ownership and state the fix and date.
4. Keep the tone formal or warm as the relationship requires.

Phrases: "I regret ...", "I apologise for ...", "We are sorry that ...".

Formal slot: "We regret the delay to your order. We will replace the item and deliver it by [day]."
Warmer slot: "I am sorry I missed our meeting. I double-booked and did not check my diary. Could we meet on [day] or [day]?"

Note: in cultures where admitting error is uncomfortable, direct ownership still tends to build trust in international business; judge the audience.

## 6. Reports, Summaries and Data

### Three-part report
1. Background: brief context.
2. Findings: factual, detailed, ordered by importance.
3. Recommendations: clear conclusions that follow from the findings.

### Process
1. Brainstorm in notes.
2. Outline the three parts.
3. Draft quickly.
4. Proofread.

### Proofreading checklist
- Spelling, punctuation, grammar.
- Most relevant data covered; less relevant material cut.
- Sentence structures varied; choppy sentences joined with conjunctions.
- Vocabulary suits topic and audience.
- Word-family variants used to avoid repetition (promote, promotion).

### Reporting transitions
- Introduce: "According to the survey ...", "It was found that ..."
- Add: furthermore, in addition. Contrast: however, despite. Compare: both, similarly.
- Cause: because, due to, as a result, consequently. Close: in summary.

## 7. Presenting and Speaking

- Prepare the core message and the likely questions.
- Take time to answer: ask for the question to be repeated; pause; if you do not know, say so and promise a follow-up.
- Attribute every source you quote.
- Chunk information into small units; use lists and diagrams to reduce load on the audience.
- Pair words with visuals rather than reading slides aloud. Treat claims about memory and eye contact as guidance to test with your own audience.
- Eye contact: look towards faces and break gaze naturally; unbroken staring can read as threatening in many professional settings, and its absence as unease. Adjust to local norms.

## 8. Persuasion Cues

- Use "you" and the person's name.
- Say "free" only when literally true.
- Give a reason with "because" whenever you ask for something; test the effect in your own channels.
- Show how the reader will feel once the problem is solved.

## 10. Grammar Checks for Professional Writing

Run these checks on any draft.

- Number: "people" is plural; "everything", "news", "advice", "weather" are singular or uncountable ("a piece of advice", not "an advice").
- "Would rather" takes the bare infinitive or past: "I would rather stay"; "I would rather you paid".
- "Be used to" takes -ing: "I am used to driving".
- Single past achievement: "managed to", not "could".
- Indirect questions keep statement order: "I asked where her parents were."
- "Must not" forbids; "need not" or "do not have to" means unnecessary.
- "Say" takes no person object; "tell" does: "She told us".
- "Because" takes a clause, "because of" a noun; never pair "because" with "so".
- "Lack" takes no preposition: "The plan lacks detail".
- "A" or "an" follows sound, not spelling: "a US citizen", "an hour".
- Titles take no article: "President Museveni", not "the President Museveni".
- No article after "the amount of" plus a plain noun where it is general; none in "at school", "in hospital".
- Adjective order: opinion before description before classification ("a lovely cool drink").
- Never combine "more" with "-er" forms; do not drop the noun after a superlative.
- Prefer the active voice; use the passive only when the doer is unknown or unimportant.
- "Actually" corrects an impression; "currently" means now.
- "Hear" is passive; "listen to" is deliberate.
- "Eventually" means after a long time; it is not "possibly".
- "Borrow from", "crash into", "whose" (possessive) versus "who's" (who is), "come from" for origin.

## 11. East African Usage Notes

1. Register fluency is the key skill: move between formal legal language and warm, human copy.
2. Avoid over-formality used to signal intelligence.
3. Pausing before an answer signals care.
4. Direct ownership of mistakes builds trust in international dealings.
5. Specific outcomes and numbers signal credibility more than abstract solution phrases.
