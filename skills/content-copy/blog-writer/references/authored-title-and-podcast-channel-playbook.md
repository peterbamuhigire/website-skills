# Authored Title and Podcast Channel Playbook

Parent skill: [blog-writer](../SKILL.md). Read when a client wants to turn an established blog into a published title (print or e-book) or a podcast, needs to decide whether audio or video suits the audience, or needs each of these channels to send readers back to the website.

Video and slide-platform distribution is in [content-distribution.md](content-distribution.md) ("YouTube as a Blog Amplifier", "SlideShare: The Underused Traffic Channel"). This file covers the published title and the podcast, plus the format-fit check that precedes any new channel.

## 1. Format-fit check (before any new channel)

Answer these before investing in a title, podcast, or video series:

1. Does the audience prefer reading, listening, or watching for this topic? Look at how comparable titles, shows, and videos perform in the niche.
2. What does the audience want from the format: step-by-step teaching, entertainment, or answers to specific questions?
3. Does the business have the source material (see section 3) and the staff time to sustain it?
4. Is there a route from the channel back to the site and the email list (sections 2 and 6)?

Proceed only when at least the first and last answers are clear.

## 2. Published title: why and how it sends traffic

A published title is among the strongest authority signals an expert can hold; readers tend to trust people who wrote the reference work on their subject. Retail marketplaces are also search engines, and their product pages often appear in web search results. Check current marketplace and search behaviour for the target market before promising either effect; do not quote sales or income figures.

Place links and calls to action in three places:

| Place | What to include |
|---|---|
| Product description | A one-line reason to visit the site and the address. More people read the description than read the title itself. |
| Inside the title | A short author note with the site address at the start or end; contextual references to site resources where they genuinely help; a closing chapter that tells readers what to do next and where. |
| Author profile on each marketplace | Site link and other products or services. |

## 3. Write the title faster from existing material

1. **Serialise first.** Break the planned title into blog posts. Publish them, collect reader questions and objections, and grow the email list before launch. Tell subscribers the launch date.
2. **Combine posts.** Existing posts on one theme become draft chapters.
3. **Transcribe other material.** Course lessons, videos, talks, and podcast episodes become text drafts.
4. **Edit and expand.** Readers expect more than collected posts: add context, stories, examples, and connecting argument. Check the marketplace's rules on content already freely available online before publishing.

## 4. Sell more copies and send readers to the site

Inside the marketplace:

1. Use the terms buyers search for in the title, subtitle, and description, without keyword stuffing or misleading claims.
2. Ask readers for honest reviews. Follow the marketplace's review policy: many prohibit reviews from family members, paid or incentivised reviews, and review swaps. Never offer anything in return for a positive review.
3. Use time-limited free or discounted promotions where the marketplace offers them. Announce the promotion to the email list and on social channels during the window. Check the current programme terms and any listing sites' rules before planning.

Outside the marketplace:

1. Optimise the title's page and the site's title page for web search.
2. Write guest articles that link to the title's page.
3. Point related content (videos, answers, slides, podcast episodes, course lessons) to the title.
4. Speak at conferences, meet-ups, and guest lectures.
5. Seek interviews with bloggers and podcasters in the niche.
6. Share on social channels and send personal, individually written emails to contacts; these outperform blind-copied mass messages.
7. Test paid social or search ads to the title's page for tightly defined audiences, within the budget rules in [traffic-channel-menu-and-evaluation.md](traffic-channel-menu-and-evaluation.md) section 2.

## 5. Podcast: when it fits

A podcast suits the business when:

- the audience listens while commuting, exercising, or working away from a screen, where text cannot reach them;
- podcast directories form a separate discovery channel from web search, so the show reaches listeners who would not find the blog;
- the niche has fewer established shows than blogs (check the directories for the niche before assuming this);
- the offer depends on trust, because hearing a voice over many episodes builds a closer relationship than text.

Minimum equipment: a computer, a reliable connection, and a decent microphone. Record in a quiet, soft-furnished room.

## 6. Start the podcast quickly

Four ways to fill the first episodes:

1. **Video to audio.** Extract the audio from existing course or video material, where it works without the picture.
2. **Text to audio.** Record blog posts read aloud, by the author or a professional voice artist. Disclose synthetic voices if used.
3. **Talk through written topics.** Discuss subjects the business has already written about, adding stories and updates.
4. **Interview guests.** The guest carries much of the conversation, often shares the episode with their own audience, and the host learns from them. Agree consent and usage terms in advance.

Every episode names one next step on the site (a resource page or sign-up), and the show notes carry the link.

## 7. Promote the podcast

1. Mention and link the show from content on other platforms.
2. Refer to relevant episodes inside related blog posts.
3. Make each episode worth recommending; quality is the most durable promotion.
4. When speaking in public, give the show's name as the single place to follow up.
5. Ask colleagues and contacts to share episodes that would genuinely interest their networks.
