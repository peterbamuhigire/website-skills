# Headline Families, Proof Placement and the Direct-Response Ethics Filter

Parent skill: [sales-copywriting](../SKILL.md). Read when generating hero
headlines, H1s, landing-page or sales-page headlines, email subject lines, or
when a draft borrows classic direct-response tactics that need an ethics check.
[Headline mastery](headline-mastery.md) holds the 4 U's scoring and templates;
this file adds the family system, the sprint, proof placement, reading-path
devices, guarantees and the filter.

## Headline families (generate across all of them)

| Family | What it does | Original slot template |
|---|---|---|
| News | Announces something genuinely new | "Now in [city]: [new service] that [specific result] in [timeframe]." |
| How-to | Promises a method | "How [audience] in [place] [achieve outcome] without [common pain]." |
| Who else / Which / Wanted | Calls out a group or makes them choose | "Which of these [n] [problems] is costing your [business type] money?" |
| Question (involvement) | Asks about the reader's interest, never the seller's | "Is your [asset or process] costing more to run than it should?" |
| Warning | Protects the reader from a costly mistake | "Read this before you sign for [category] in [country]." |
| Reason-why | Gives specific reasons | "[Number] reasons [audience] are moving to [solution] this [period]." |
| Testimonial | Lets a real client make the claim | "'[Specific result in the client's words],' says [name], [role], [town]." |
| Story or first person | Opens a narrative | "I lost three tenders before I changed one page of our website." |
| Offer or price | Leads with a real deal (commodity or offer campaigns only) | "[Offer] for the first [n] [audience] who [action] by [date]." |
| Admit a weakness | Buys belief with candour | "We're not the cheapest [category] in [city]. Here's why [audience] still choose us." |
| Hidden benefit | Surfaces the indirect outcome buyers want most | "[Service] that also [valued side effect, e.g. frees your Saturdays]." |
| Before and after | Contrasts wrong and right | "The wrong way and the right way to choose [category] in [country]." |

Rules:

- Formulas are prompts, not substitutes for an idea.
- A headline must carry a benefit, news, useful curiosity or a quick solution.
  Curiosity alone rarely works; pair it with news or self-interest.
- **"Dull to whom?"** Plain category language ("Handling acid spills in food
  factories") grips the right specialist reader. Do not decorate it.
- **Specific or intriguing? Test both.** A precise figure can shrink the
  audience to people for whom that figure matters; an intriguing promise widens
  it.
- Put a brand or product name inside a strong headline, never as its whole
  subject.
- No puns or clever lines that hide the benefit unless tested against a plain
  benefit headline.
- Sentence case or title case, never all capitals.

## 60-minute headline sprint

1. Write down the top benefit, the hidden benefit, the main fear, the news angle
   and the strongest verified proof number.
2. Generate at least five candidates per family (50 or more in total).
3. Run each through these questions: What is the strongest benefit and offer?
   Is it about the prospect or about us? Could a competitor use it unchanged
   (too generic)? Will it bore? Did we think, or fill space?
4. **Standalone test:** if the headline ran alone with only a response
   instruction, would it pull?
5. Shortlist five; test the top three where traffic allows (see
   `experimentation`); otherwise choose by evidence and record the reason.

## Proof placement: early, specific, attributed

- A big promise creates doubt; answer it immediately. Place the first proof
  (testimonial, result, independent review, credential) close to the headline,
  not at the end of the page.
- Use at least three testimonials so they do not look selected; make each
  specific, with a result, from someone like the prospect, with name, role and
  organisation where the person has consented.
- Tag each testimonial by the objection it answers (price, trust, "will it work
  for me?", delivery time) and place it next to that claim.
- Client-voiced endorsements (a satisfied client describing the result in their
  own words) can say what the business cannot credibly say about itself; they
  need the client's permission and review rights.

## Reading-path devices for long pages

- The first sentence's only job is to get the second sentence read.
- Subheads alone must tell the whole offer to a skimmer.
- End sections with a bridge that pulls the reader on ("Here's how it works.",
  "Look at the numbers below."). Avoid empty bridges that promise significance
  without delivering it.
- Close the loop: the final line returns to the benefit that opened the page.

## Guarantees and risk reversal for services

- Prefer **process guarantees** to results or income guarantees: "If we miss the
  agreed launch date, next month's care-plan fee is waived."
- Make guarantees generous, named, repeated near the price and on the order or
  enquiry form, and always honoured.
- Results or income guarantees can breach consumer-protection and
  financial-promotion rules; check the client's jurisdiction before using one.

## Direct-response ethics filter (mandatory)

Classic direct-response books contain tactics that are now misleading or
unlawful in many markets. Reject or rewrite:

| Tactic | Why it fails | Replacement |
|---|---|---|
| Quotation marks around the advertiser's own words ("unattributed testimonial") | Implies a third-party endorsement that does not exist | Real, consented testimonials only |
| Ads or pages disguised as editorial or news | Hidden advertising | Clear "Advertisement" or sponsor labelling |
| Manufactured scarcity or deadlines; phantom price rises | False urgency | Real, provable limits with the true reason |
| Unsubstantiated health, income or business-opportunity claims | Regulated in most markets | Substantiated claims only; sector rules checked (health products, betting, financial promotions) |
| Daily unsolicited follow-up, guilt or invented third-party pressure | Breaches data-protection and messaging norms; damages premium positioning | Consent-based, value-adding, capped follow-up with opt-out |
| Fake reviews, bought followers, staff posing as customers | Deceptive; prohibited under several regimes | Genuine early advocates, truthful "founding client" framing |
| Calling an old product "new" | Misleading | "New" only for genuinely new or improved offers |
| Hype register (all capitals, stacked exclamation marks, "cash machine") | Reads as unprofessional to premium buyers | Calm, specific, evidence-led language |

Keep the durable craft: offer first, specific proof early, reason-why, clear
single call to action, real guarantees.
