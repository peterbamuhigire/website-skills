# UX Strategy: Differentiation, Value Innovation and Meaning

Parent skill: [premium-ui-ux-design](../SKILL.md). Read when challenging a client brief, deciding how a premium website or digital product will differ from its category, or when a saturated market no longer rewards "more features".

Use alongside `levy-four-tenets.md` (the four-tenet formula, the four misinterpretations and the ten not-strategies). This file supplies the decision rules and workshop procedures those tenets depend on.

## 1. Choose the competitive path before designing

1. Name the path the client is actually on:
   - **Cost leadership**: winning by being cheapest in the category. UX work supports efficiency and scale; premium design fees are hard to justify.
   - **Differentiation**: winning on something buyers value enough to pay more for. This is where UX differentiation earns its keep.
2. If the client claims differentiation, write the differentiator in one sentence and name the user behaviour that proves buyers value it. If neither can be written, the brief is not ready.
3. Treat a UX differentiator as a change to how the task is done (a constraint, a new interaction, a trust mechanism), not a visual style.
4. State the stage:
   - **New product**: strategy aims at enough product/market fit to fund growth or capture share. Prioritise validation over build.
   - **Mature product**: strategy extends the core value proposition while protecting what already works. Prioritise continuity, migration and measured change.

## 2. Value innovation check

Value innovation is the simultaneous pursuit of differentiation and lower cost, producing a clear leap in value for both buyer and business. Run this check on any concept that claims to be new:

| Question | Pass condition |
|---|---|
| Which category rule does this break? | A specific convention is named (for example, "strangers cannot be trusted in your home"). |
| What mental model must users change? | The change is stated, and the UX mechanism that makes the change feel safe is named. |
| How is trust built into the experience itself? | Profiles, verification, reviews, guarantees or equivalent are part of the core flow, not a footer badge. |
| Is the market two-sided? | If yes, both sides have a designed experience and a reason to return. |
| Where does the experience continue offline? | Offline fulfilment and the return to the interface (reviews, receipts, rebooking) are mapped. |
| How does the business earn from the leap in value? | A revenue mechanism is tied to the moment of value, not bolted on. |
| Is it unique, or at least clearly better than current alternatives? | If neither, stop and return to competitor analysis. |

Classify the innovation:

- **Sustaining**: better performance for existing customers of the incumbent. Compete on execution quality.
- **Disruptive**: starts with simpler, cheaper use at the bottom of a market that incumbents ignore, then moves up. Expect lower initial margins and early users who are not the incumbent's best customers.

## 3. Evidence rule: no Field-of-Dreams launches

- Do not accept "if we build it, they will come" as a plan. Stakeholders routinely assume value instead of verifying it.
- Before scaling any build, confront target customers with something they can react to: a problem interview, a landing page, a concierge service or a prototype (see `../../../launch-ops/experimentation/references/pre-build-validation-and-rollout-playbook.md` and `../../website-experience-mapping/references/validation-research-method-playbook.md`).
- Validated research means a specific customer segment has shown, through direct interaction, that it finds value in the offer. Observation and empathy work alone do not meet that bar.
- When there is no budget for validation, ask for one or two extra weeks and state the risk of proceeding without them in writing. If the answer is no, record the decision and the unvalidated assumptions in the strategy brief.

## 4. Killer UX behaviours to require of the design lead

Check that the lead designer:

1. Works with stakeholders from inception and designs structured experiments rather than relying on hunches.
2. Identifies the few key moments that must be excellent (storyboard them) and poaches proven patterns from competitors and non-competitors.
3. Studies the existing market to find exploitable UX gaps.
4. Talks directly to prospective users or current power users to confirm the primary utility.
5. Designs the whole journey across online and offline touchpoints, including the loop back to the interface.

## 5. Strategist qualities and stakeholder reach

- Look for critical thinking, comfort with risk and failure, empathy for stakeholder motives as well as user needs, and persistence in asking "why".
- Build strategy from the collective view of the business and its customers. Interview unexpected internal stakeholders (reception, sales assistants, dispatch, support) as well as executives; they see failure points leaders do not.

## 6. Solution innovation versus meaning innovation

Two kinds of innovation need different processes:

| | Solution innovation | Meaning innovation |
|---|---|---|
| Changes | A better answer to an existing question | Which question is worth answering |
| Method | Outside-in: user research, Lean UX, jobs-to-be-done, A/B testing | Inside-out: the team's own envisioning, sharpened by criticism |
| Role of customers | Source of problems and validation of solutions | Tested last, to check a formed framework, never to source it |
| Risk in crowded markets | More ideas add noise | Lack of direction |

Decision rules:

- In an overcrowded category the scarce resource is direction, not ideas. Do not answer stagnation with a longer backlog.
- User-centred research validates how a meaning is implemented; it cannot generate a new meaning, because customers rarely articulate one.
- Alternate waves: after a meaning shift, run solution-innovation cycles to refine it; when refinements stop moving the category, schedule a meaning cycle. A team that only ships refinements eventually loses the category.

### Envisioning sentence

Each participant completes:

> I would love a [solution] that enables me to [experience] because [meaning].

The clause after "because" is the deliverable. Reject sentences whose "because" clause restates a feature.

### Five-stage inside-out process

1. **Individuals** (several weeks): each participant envisions alone and drafts hypotheses using the sentence above.
2. **Pairs**: sparring partners merge and sharpen hypotheses through criticism.
3. **Radical circle** (a two-day workshop of roughly 15–20 people): pairs present; the group first agrees what it dislikes about the current meaning (the "common enemy"), then builds a new meaning from the clash between two well-formed visions rather than by voting on many ideas.
4. **Interpreters** (six to eight outside experts, brought in part-way through): choose people who study the same customers' lives through a different lens, preferably from outside the industry. Their deliverable is a sharper question, not an idea.
5. **Customers** (last): test whether the framework resonates and how it should be implemented.

### Website application

- Use the envisioning sentence to write the homepage promise and brand story only after the meaning is agreed.
- Record the chosen meaning in the strategy brief so page architecture, proof and copy can be checked against it.
