# Brand Story Spine: Roles, Drivers and Plots

Parent skill: [brand-storytelling](../SKILL.md). Read when writing or auditing an About page, founder or origin story, vision section, case-study narrative or home-page story block, and the approved brand brief does not yet say which role the brand plays in the customer's life, which human motive the story serves, or which plot shape fits.

Complements, and does not replace:

- [SB7 BrandScript worksheet](sb7-brandscript-worksheet.md), which fixes the customer as hero and the brand as guide and builds the seven-part message. This file refines the guide position: "guide" is one of eight roles, and the role must match how the audience sees itself.
- [Narrative spine and emotional arc](narrative-spine-and-emotional-arc.md), which plans the story across pages. This file supplies the three choices that sit underneath it.
- The brand archetype chosen in `brand-strategy`. An archetype describes the brand's personality and voice; a **role** describes what the brand does in the customer's life. Keep both, and check they do not contradict each other.

## 1. The spine card

Complete one card per brand (and one per major audience if they differ). Every line needs evidence.

| Line | Question | Evidence required |
|---|---|---|
| Audience self-image | How does the audience see itself in relation to this need? | Interview or review quotations |
| Brand role | Which of the eight roles does the brand play? | Role test passed (section 2) |
| Human driver | Which motive does the story serve? | Buyer language showing the motive |
| Plot | Which plot shape fits the true story? | Verified events |
| Proof | What shows the brand actually plays this role? | Named, consented evidence |
| Ownable claim | What can this brand claim that rivals cannot credibly claim in the same way? | Comparison with the top three alternatives |
| Page action | What should the reader do after the story? | Page brief |

Three-sentence test of the finished card: "[Audience] wants [outcome]. The obstacle is [obstacle]. [Brand] helps because [reason to believe]." If you cannot write it from the card, the card is incomplete.

## 2. Brand roles and the self-image test

| Role | What the brand does | Fits when |
|---|---|---|
| Guide | Shows the way | The customer wants to do it themselves, with direction |
| Helper | Takes care of a need | The customer wants the task off their plate |
| Guardian | Protects health, money, property or community | Risk and trust dominate (insurance, SACCOs, fintech, security, healthcare) |
| Coach | Motivates and holds to a standard | Progress depends on the customer's own effort (training, fitness, education) |
| Catalyst | Sparks a change the customer already wants | The customer is ready but stuck |
| Alchemist | Transforms the customer into who they aspire to be | Identity and aspiration drive the purchase (fashion, beauty, professional development) |
| Entertainer | Transports and delights | Leisure, hospitality, events |
| Reliever | Eases pain or difficulty | The need is urgent or distressing (repairs, medical, legal crisis) |

Self-image test: the role must fit how the audience sees itself, not only what the brand does. If the audience sees itself as the carer, the provider or the expert, casting the brand in that same role takes something from them. A retailer that presents itself as the family's "caregiver" can offend parents who see caring as their own job; "helper" fits better.

Procedure:

1. From interviews and reviews, write one sentence on how the audience describes its own role ("I am the one who keeps this family's money safe").
2. List the roles that would compete with that self-image and rule them out.
3. Choose the role that supports it. Most service brands for East African SMEs sit best as guide, helper or guardian.
4. Check the role against the proof. A brand cannot claim "guardian" without evidence of protection (guarantees, security practice, a track record of handling problems).

## 3. Human drivers

Choose one primary driver, and at most one secondary. Each has a test question the story must answer "yes" to.

| Driver | Test question |
|---|---|
| Survival and health | Does it support health, stamina or long-term security? |
| Safety | Does it give peace of mind or reduce risk? |
| Pleasure | Does it offer enjoyment? |
| Community | Does it build belonging? |
| Love and family | Does it help people care for, or be closer to, those they love? |
| Self-esteem | Does it promise real improvement? |
| Status | Does it add standing or approval? |
| Rivalry | Does it give a competitive edge? |
| Influence | Does it give more say or control? |
| Convenience | Does it save time or effort? |
| Development | Does it add knowledge or expertise? |
| Exhilaration | Does it promise excitement? |

Select the driver from buyer language, not from the brand's wishes. A clinic may want to talk about technology; its patients may be driven by safety and family.

## 4. Plot shapes

Use the shortest shape the true story supports. Never force events into a plot.

| Plot | Shape | Web use |
|---|---|---|
| Overcoming the obstacle | A capable underdog faces a large problem and wins | Case studies about beating a deadline, a regulator's requirement or a larger competitor |
| Rags to riches | Gain, near-loss, lasting success | Founder stories, only where every stage is verified |
| The quest | A goal, obstacles on the way, the prize | Service journeys and multi-phase projects |
| Voyage and return | Into the unknown, home changed | Diaspora return, relocation, entering a new market |
| Rebirth | Transformation through difficulty | Turnarounds and rebrands, told with the client's consent |

Situation, choice, action, result (the parent skill's default) remains the right shape for most case studies. Use the shapes above when the verified story genuinely has that arc.

## 5. Community as hero (East African rule)

In Uganda, Kenya and the wider region, stories that cast a company or donor as the saviour of a community read as the old development and NGO pattern, and they cost trust.

1. The community, customer or client organisation is the protagonist. The brand is the guide, helper or guardian.
2. Show what people did, decided and achieved, not only what the brand gave.
3. Name places and groups accurately and with permission. Do not use a community's hardship as decoration.
4. Imagery follows the same rule: no poverty imagery, no people shown only as recipients. Route photography and casting decisions to `design-system-skills`.
5. Purpose and giving claims appear only when the action is funded and sustained. One-off gestures presented as purpose damage credibility.
6. Quest and rags-to-riches stories (starting a business, moving from village to city, returning from the diaspora) resonate strongly in the region. That makes verification more important, not less.

## 6. Ownable claim

A claim is ownable when the brand can hold it credibly even if rivals offer the same function. Test it:

- The top three alternatives buyers compare do not already say it.
- The brand's own record proves it.
- It links to the chosen role and driver.
- It survives being said plainly in one sentence.

If no claim passes, do not invent one. Use the most specific verified fact the brand has, and log the gap for `brand-strategy`.

## 7. Vision section test

When an About page or home page carries a vision statement, it must:

1. name a real, significant problem for a defined group;
2. be specific enough that no competitor could paste it onto their site;
3. say, with equal weight, how it will be achieved: the enabling capability and the first proof.

Template: "Within [horizon], we intend [outcome] for [group]. It is achievable now because [enabler]; our first proof is [verified result]."

## 8. Before and after (illustrative)

- Generic (brand as hero): "We are transforming lives across Uganda through our innovative savings solutions and commitment to excellence."
- Spine applied (guardian role, safety and family drivers, community as hero): "Market vendors in Owino save small amounts every day, often with nowhere safe to keep them. Since 2019 our members have saved through a phone they already own, and they decide together how the group's loans are used. We keep the money safe and the records open; the decisions stay theirs."

## 9. Worked example (illustrative)

A Kira paediatric dental practice. Interviews show parents see themselves as the ones who protect their children; they fear pain and long drives into town. Role: helper (not guardian, which would compete with the parents' self-image). Drivers: family (primary) and convenience (secondary). Plot: situation, choice, action, result for case stories; no founder rags-to-riches, because the verified history is ordinary. Ownable claim: the only practice north of a named suburb with paediatric equipment (verified against the practice list). About lead: "You look after your child's teeth every day. We make the check-up easy: close to home, with equipment made for small mouths, and a dentist who explains each step to your child first."

## 10. Acceptance checklist

- [ ] The audience's self-image is recorded from real quotations.
- [ ] The brand role supports that self-image and is backed by proof.
- [ ] One primary driver, chosen from buyer language.
- [ ] The plot fits verified events; nothing is dramatised.
- [ ] The community or customer is the protagonist; the brand is not the saviour.
- [ ] Any purpose or giving claim is backed by funded, continuing action.
- [ ] The ownable claim passes the four tests or is replaced by a specific verified fact.
- [ ] The three-sentence test can be written from the card.
