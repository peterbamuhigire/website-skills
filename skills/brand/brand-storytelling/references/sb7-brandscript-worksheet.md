# Customer-Hero Message Worksheet (BrandScript)

Parent skill: [brand-storytelling](../SKILL.md). Read when building or auditing a brand's story-based message architecture (the seven-part BrandScript), writing hero sections and home pages from it, or when `brand-strategy`, `sales-copywriting` or `page-builder` needs the completed BrandScript as input.

Principle: the customer is the hero and the brand is the guide. Fill every field from customer evidence (interviews, sales calls, reviews, support logs), never from internal opinion. Record the source and date beside each answer.

## Worksheet Order

Work through the stages in this order; the seven story elements are marked.

1. Gather evidence
2. Define the customer's want (element: Character)
3. Map the obstacles (element: Problem)
4. Set the brand's credibility (element: Guide)
5. Set the route (element: Plan)
6. Set the invitations (element: Call to action)
7. Set the stakes and the outcome (elements: Failure, Success)
8. Define the identity shift (extra element: Transformation)
9. Apply to pages, then audit

## 1. Gather Evidence

Collect at least five customer conversations or review sets. Extract, in the customer's own words: what they wanted, what got in the way, how it felt, what convinced them, what they did first, and what changed after. Note the language to reuse.

## 2. The Customer's Want

Choose one desire related to the offer. Several desires blur the message; one drives action.

| Area | Example of a want |
|---|---|
| Money | Earn more, spend less, feel secure |
| Time | Save hours, simplify work |
| Standing | Be respected, belong to a peer group |
| Relationships | Connection, support |
| Purpose | Leave something lasting, do meaningful work |
| Giving | Help others, contribute |

Output: one sentence in the customer's words. Slot: "I want [outcome] so that [reason]."

## 3. Map the Obstacles

Describe the problem at three levels:

| Level | Question |
|---|---|
| Outward | What is practically going wrong? |
| Inward | How does that make the customer feel about themselves? |
| Principle | Why is it unfair that anyone should face this? |

The inward level is usually the real motivator; naming it produces "they understand me". The principle level turns a transaction into a cause.

Choose one **villain**: a root cause the customer recognises at once (a broken process, a wasteful habit, a harmful norm). Rules: cause, not symptom; one only; real, not invented; never a person or group.

## 4. Set the Brand's Credibility

Order matters: empathy, then authority.

- **Empathy line:** "We know how it feels to [inward problem]." Place it in the hero area before product detail; use "you" more than "we".
- **Authority proof** (choose the strongest, show without boasting): customer testimonials; specific verified figures; credentials, certificates, press; a visible demonstration of the product or process working.
- Do not open with company history or credentials before the customer's problem has been acknowledged.

## 5. Set the Route

- **Process route (before buying):** at most three steps. Slot: "[Book or start] then [we do the key work] then [you get the outcome]."
- **Commitment route (after buying):** named promises that answer customer fears (no hidden fees, reply times, refund terms). Each must be a policy the business honours.
- Give the route a name ("The Three-Step Setup") so it feels concrete.

## 6. Set the Invitations

| Type | Purpose | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | Leads to a sale or the first step | Buy, book, register, call, request a quote |
| Soft | Builds trust with those not yet ready | Free guide, webinar, trial, assessment, case study |

Placement: direct CTA in the header, in the hero and again after each proof point. Keep one consistent label and style. Soft CTAs show expertise, earn goodwill and position the brand as guide before purchase. Every touchpoint offers both: "Ready? [Direct]. Not sure yet? [Soft]."

## 7. Stakes and Outcome

### Stakes (use lightly)
A story needs something to lose. Losses often motivate more than equal gains, so name what inaction costs; treat this as a hypothesis to test.

Sequence: establish a vulnerability with a verified, dated fact; show it applies to this reader; present the solution; give the action.
Limit: two or three stakes per page. Too much fear switches readers off; none produces indifference. Aim for urgency, not fear.
Formats: three "Are you struggling with ...?" bullets; one problem paragraph in an email; a calm "Do not let [consequence] happen" line.

### Outcome
Do not assume readers can picture the improved life; show it. Choose the outcome type that fits the audience:
1. Standing: access, an identity such as "certified" or "member", aspirational language.
2. Relief: less worry, less workload, more time.
3. Fulfilment: inspiration, acceptance as they are, part of something larger.

**Before-and-after grid** for each key page:

| Dimension | Without us | With us |
|---|---|---|
| Have | [practical problem] | [practical result] |
| Feel | [inward frustration] | [emotional result] |
| Typical day | [current pain] | [improved day] |
| Standing | [current identity] | [aspired identity] |

Show real customers in the "after" state, not abstract graphics or anonymous stock photography. Art direction: `design-system-skills`.

## 8. Identity Shift

Define who the customer becomes, not only where they end up. Slot: "From [current identity] to [aspired identity]." Prompts: how do they want friends to describe them? What kind of person do they want to become?

Affirm it after success (purchase, onboarding, first result) with a message, success page or milestone that names who they have become. Affirmation converts customers into advocates.

## 9. Apply to the Website

1. Above the fold, one short line (about ten words) stating the identity aspired to, the problem solved or what you do. Do not lead with the company name, slogan or category.
2. Direct CTA in header and hero, repeated after proof, styled consistently.
3. Images of success featuring real customers.
4. One primary offer and at most two alternatives; lead with the one that solves the most urgent problem.
5. Few words: headlines, subheads, short bullets; cut any line that does not serve the BrandScript.

## One-Page Template

```
Brand:
Customer wants:             [one desire, customer's words]
Villain:                    [one root cause]
Outward / Inward / Principle problem:
Empathy line:               "We know how it feels to ..."
Authority proof:            [testimonial] [figure] [credential] [demonstration]
Process route (max 3):      1. ... 2. ... 3. ...     Route name:
Commitments:                [promise] [promise] [promise]
Direct CTA:                 Soft CTA:
Stakes (2 to 3):
Outcome type:               standing | relief | fulfilment
Before/after grid:          Have / Feel / Day / Standing
Identity shift:             From ... to ...
Hero line:                  [problem] + [solution] + [outcome]
Evidence sources:           [interviews, reviews, calls, with dates]
```

Store the completed BrandScript in the project brand brief so it feeds hero copy, sales pages and scripts.

## Audit Checklist

- [ ] The customer, not the brand, is the hero on every key page.
- [ ] One desire, one villain, three problem levels named.
- [ ] Empathy precedes authority and product detail.
- [ ] Every proof claim and figure is verified and dated.
- [ ] Route has three steps or fewer and a name; commitments are honoured policies.
- [ ] Every page carries a direct and a soft CTA.
- [ ] Stakes limited to two or three, and sourced.
- [ ] Outcome shown explicitly with a grid and real imagery.
- [ ] Aspired identity defined and affirmed after purchase.
