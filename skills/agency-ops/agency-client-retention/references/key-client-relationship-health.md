# Key-Client Relationship Health

Parent skill: [agency-client-retention](../SKILL.md). Read when managing the
agency's few strategic clients (the AA grade and flagship accounts), when a
large client relationship depends on one person, when appointing a senior
sponsor for an account, or when recovering trust after a delivery failure.

The Client Health Scorecard in `legacy-guidance.md` gives a monthly pulse for
every client. This file adds a deeper layer for the handful of accounts whose
loss would hurt the agency: where the relationship stands, how strong it is,
how many people hold it together, and how value is proven each quarter.

## 1. Choose which clients get this treatment

Select on strategic importance, not size alone. Score each candidate:

- profit-growth potential over three years;
- current strength of the agency's position with the client;
- reference value (a recognised name in the niche, an opinion leader);
- willingness to try new work with you;
- relationship strength at senior level.

Then place each on a two-by-two of **growth potential** and **agency strength**:

| Type | Potential | Strength | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strategic | High | High | Senior lead, joint annual plan, sponsor, quarterly value review |
| Star | High | Lower | Pursuit budget of senior time; build position deliberately |
| Status | Lower | High | Delivery excellence and margin protection |
| Streamline | Lower | Lower | Productised lower-touch tier, or a managed exit |

Review the list once a year and remove key status when circumstances change.
Watch concentration: if one client carries a large share of agency revenue,
record it as a risk and plan diversification.

## 2. Place the relationship on the typology

Two questions: who depends on whom, and is the exchange transactional or
relational?

| Position | Dependence | Exchange | Risk and move |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vulnerable supplier | Agency depends on client | Transactional | Client can squeeze fees. Diversify the book; build tools or data the client relies on; propose shared targets |
| Vulnerable client | Client depends on agency | Transactional | Client may resent lock-in. Offer open documentation and exit paths (see `post-launch-retention-playbook.md`) |
| Inviting client | Agency depends on client | Relational | A powerful client chooses to collaborate. Honour it with reliability |
| Inviting agency | Client depends on agency | Relational | Choose to invest in mutual benefit rather than use power |
| Competitive | Mutual | Transactional | Annual re-tender, win-lose. Move towards joint planning |
| Collaborative partnership | Mutual | Relational | The target for strategic clients |

Record the current position, the target position, the timeline and the actions
agreed with the client.

## 3. Measure relationship quality

Relationship quality combines **managed conflict**, **trust** (both
competence and goodwill) and **commitment** (investment specific to this
relationship). Four drivers move it:

1. **Shared goals.** Jointly defined success measures tied to the client's
   priorities, reviewed regularly.
2. **Information sharing.** Early warning of problems, roadmap updates, market
   insight offered without being asked.
3. **Reciprocity.** Visible give-and-take; shared risk where authorised.
4. **Personal ties.** People on both sides know each other beyond the agenda.

**Relationship survey (7-point agree–disagree; ask both sides and compare).**
Original items, written for this engine:

- We agree on what success looks like for this work this year.
- They tell us early when something changes on their side.
- We tell them early when something is going wrong on ours.
- When one of us goes beyond the contract, the other side notices and returns it.
- Disagreements are about how to reach our shared goal, not about blame.
- We would trust them with confidential information about our plans.
- People on both sides would take a call from each other outside meeting times.
- We have invested time or tools that only make sense for this relationship.

Large gaps between the two sides' scores matter more than the averages.

## 4. Multi-threading check

A relationship held by one person breaks when that person leaves. For each key
client, count active relationships by level and function:

| Level | Client-side names | Agency-side owner | Last meaningful contact |
|---|---|---|---|
| Owner, MD or board | | | |
| Director (marketing, digital, finance) | | | |
| Manager (day-to-day) | | | |
| Operator (site editors, front office) | | | |

Flag an account as **single-threaded** if all contact runs through one person,
and as **no senior tie** if no one at director level or above knows the agency.
Either flag triggers a named action within the quarter.

## 5. The executive sponsor

For each strategic client, name a senior person at the agency (founder,
partner or director) as sponsor.

**Role:** strategic and social, not tactical. The sponsor meets the client's
senior leader at least twice a year, removes internal barriers, and backs the
account lead. The sponsor does not haggle over deliverables or bypass the
account lead; doing so teaches the client to go around the lead and can signal
a lack of confidence in them.

**Commitment (a practitioner heuristic; set your own):** roughly 10–12 hours a
year per account, plus a short monthly check-in with the account lead.

**Set-up steps:**

1. Write a one-paragraph sponsor profile for the account (seniority, sector
   fit, relationships).
2. Agree expectations in writing: hours, roles, what the sponsor will and will
   not do.
3. Before each sponsor meeting, the account lead sends a briefing: context,
   objectives, who attends, talking points, topics to avoid (no pricing), the
   desired next step.
4. Report to the sponsor monthly or quarterly: key results, wins, risks,
   **decisions required**, resources needed.
5. Plan succession for both the sponsor and the account lead.

**Escalation note (account lead to sponsor):** issue, impact on the client,
options with pros and cons, recommendation, decision deadline. Never escalate a
problem without options.

**Local adaptation.** In family-owned groups and parastatals in Uganda and
Kenya, senior-to-senior contact often carries more weight. Plan sponsor visits
around meaningful moments (year-end gatherings, AGMs, launches) and keep the
account lead visibly in charge. Check the client's gifts and hospitality policy
before any hospitality, especially with public bodies.

## 6. Quarterly value-tracking session

Hold with the client's senior decision-maker. Value must be stated against a
baseline agreed at the start of the engagement.

1. Baseline recap: where the agreed measures stood when the work began.
2. What moved this quarter, with sources and dates.
3. Value realised and how it is attributed (be explicit about what else
   changed, such as a price change or a new campaign).
4. Issues and recovery actions, with owners.
5. New opportunities (screened with the need-versus-wish test in
   `account-cube-expansion-sequence.md`).
6. Joint priorities for next quarter.
7. Decisions needed from each side.

Never promise outcomes the evidence cannot support; mark unmeasured items
`not assessed`.

## 7. Trust-recovery protocol

When the agency misses a commitment:

1. **Acknowledge within 24 hours** and take ownership. No excuses in the first
   message.
2. **State the impact** on the client in their terms.
3. **Issue a recovery plan** with steps, owners and dates.
4. **Send progress updates** at the promised times.
5. **Share a root-cause review** with the fix to the process, not only the
   instance.
6. **Rebuild through consistent follow-through** before proposing any
   expansion.

Phrase bank: "We missed [commitment]. The impact on you was [impact]. Here is
what we are doing, who owns it, and when you will hear from us next."
