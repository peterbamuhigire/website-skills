# Objection Root Cause and Lost-Deal Debrief

Parent skill: [premium-sales-conversation](../SKILL.md). Read when the same
objection keeps recurring, when win rates fall, when reviewing lost deals each
quarter, or when writing proposal and website FAQs that pre-empt objections.

`premium-objection-handling.md` gives the in-call pattern (acknowledge →
reframe → evidence → confirm) and scripts by objection type. This file works
upstream: it asks why the objection happens at all and fixes the cause.

## 1. Three root causes

If you are in front of the right prospect, a recurring objection has one of two
causes. A third sits before both.

| Root cause | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| 0. Prospecting | The prospect could never afford, need or decide on the work | Tighten qualification, pre-disqualify by range, change lead sources |
| 1. Positioning | The prospect does not see you as the expert ("why should we pay more for you?") | Build earned authority: case studies, talks, publications, introductions |
| 2. Presentation | The prospect did not see value, proof or fit | Rework the presentation using the six building blocks in `price-presentation-and-choice-close.md` |

Fix upstream. Rehearsing more comebacks to an objection caused by poor
prospecting wastes effort.

fewer objections, not better comebacks.

## 3. Break the pattern

- If the same opening move keeps producing the same objection, change the
  opening move.
- For each persistent objection, brainstorm about 20 possible answers and
  10–20 questions. Answering with a good question is often stronger than a
  statement: "Compared with what?", "What would you need to see to be
  confident this pays for itself?", "If price were equal, which would you
  choose, and why?"
- Ask colleagues who handle the objection well what they say.
- **Pre-empt in writing.** Put the top three objections into the proposal and
  website FAQ: "Clients usually ask three things before they commit: [q1],
  [q2], [q3]. Here are straight answers." Commercial wording in proposals is
  owned by the proposal engine (proposal-skills).

## 4. The cost-of-cheap reframe (price as a value gap)

Except for true commodities, buyers do not choose on lowest price alone. A
price objection usually means the value difference has not been shown.

1. Ask whether the prospect is treating the work as a commodity.
2. Make the difference concrete: what the cheaper option leaves out, how long
   it lasts, the risk of failure.
3. Show the cost of the cheaper choice: rework, lost enquiries, rebuild.
4. Restate the value accepted earlier in the meeting.

Example: "A cheaper site is possible. What it usually leaves out is the enquiry
tracking you told me the board needs. Without it you're back to guessing which
adverts work. Which matters more this year: the lower fee, or knowing your cost
per enquiry?"

## 5. Lost-deal debrief

Call two or three lost prospects each quarter. Make clear you are not reopening
the decision.

**Opening:** "Thank you for considering us. I'm not calling to change your
decision; I'm trying to get better. Could you give me 15 minutes of honest
feedback on where our proposal fell short for you?"

**Questions:**

- What made the option you chose feel safer?
- What did we say that made you doubt us?
- What did we leave unanswered?
- If you were advising us, what would you change first?

For public tenders, also use any formal debriefing right the procurement rules
give losing bidders; confirm the current procedure with the proposal engine
before relying on it.

**Write the debrief with specific, temporary causes, never permanent blame.**

| Avoid | Write instead |
|---|---|
| "They just picked the cheapest." | "Our method section scored below the winner on clarity. Next bid: a one-page method diagram and a named-risk table." |
| "They don't value quality." | "We led with features; next time lead with their cost of delay." |

Feed each finding into the register (§2) with an owner.

## 6. Mindset rules for the sales team

- Treat objections as clues to what the prospect still needs, not as attacks.
- Take responsibility for the case you made; blaming the market or the
  prospect gives up the power to change the result.
- Ask plainly for a decision and accept a clear "no" calmly. Wanting to be
  liked more than wanting a decision leads to timid closes.
- No pressure beyond the facts, no fear imagery the facts do not support, and
  no pushing buyers into debt.
