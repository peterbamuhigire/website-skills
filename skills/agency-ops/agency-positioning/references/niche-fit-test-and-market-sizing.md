# Niche Fit Test and Market Sizing

Parent skill: [agency-positioning](../SKILL.md). Read when a shortlisted niche
has passed the market-size guardrail in `niche-selection-rubric.md` and you
need to test whether its buyers will actually respond and pay, when a niche
label feels too broad, or when an East African niche looks too small inside one
country.

`niche-selection-rubric.md` decides the specialisation type, the size band and
the score. This file adds four buyer-behaviour questions, a narrowing rule, a
geography sequence and a way to size niches across borders.

## 1. Settle the model before the marketing

Decide what you sell, to whom, at what fee and whether it recurs before you
build any funnel. Marketing an unclear model wastes the effort. Hold the
decision with the "five ones" discipline: one target market, one lead-generation
method, one conversion mechanism, one core programme, held for one year. Each
extra niche, programme or channel multiplies complexity rather than adding to
it.

## 2. The four-question fit test

Answer each with evidence, not opinion.

| # | Question | How to test | Fail signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Self-identification.** Will a member read your headline and think "this is for me"? | Write the headline naming the group ("Websites for private clinics in Kampala"). Show it to five people in the group. | They describe themselves with a different word, or say "that's sort of us" |
| 2 | **Propensity to invest.** Do they already pay to win customers? | List visible spend: search ads, directories, radio, billboards, newspapers, social ads, property portals, trade fairs. Check paid-search competition in a keyword tool. | They rely wholly on word of mouth, or customers do not choose providers by searching |
| 3 | **Enough of them at the right size.** | Count firms at the target size in the reachable geography from registries, association rolls and directories. Build a named list (see the 200-name viability test in the rubric). | You cannot name enough firms that could afford the fee |
| 4 | **An association or gathering point.** | Find the association, federation, regulator register or annual event. | No list, no events, no shared forum |

Add two tie-breakers:

- **Founder fit.** Does the niche connect to the founder's background, so that
  the niche partly selects you?
- **Transaction value.** Is one extra customer worth many times the monthly
  fee to the client? High-value sectors (legal, private healthcare, schools,
  real estate, hospitality, automotive) pass easily.

## 3. Go a specialty within the specialty

If the label covers very different businesses, narrow it.

| Too broad | Narrower | Narrowest workable |
|---|---|---|
| Healthcare | Private clinics | Fertility and maternity clinics |
| Construction | Real-estate developers | Developers selling off-plan apartments to the diaspora |
| Education | Private schools | Boarding secondary schools competing for Term 1 admissions |
| Hospitality | Hotels | Lodges selling mid-week conference packages |

Stop narrowing when the §2 count falls below what the business needs. Then
widen geography rather than the definition.

## 4. Geography: own the backyard first

1. **Win two or three proof clients close to home**, where you can meet them,
   visit their premises and see results first-hand.
2. **Then widen**: national, then regional (EAC), then diaspora-facing, using
   the local proof.
3. **Consider smaller towns.** Firms outside the capital often face less search
   competition, so results come sooner and proof accumulates faster. Test this
   in your own market before relying on it.

## 5. Sizing a niche across borders

A single East African country rarely has thousands of firms in a narrow niche.
Use one or more of these adaptations and record which you used:

| Adaptation | When | Evidence to collect |
|---|---|---|
| **Go regional** | The buyer type exists across the EAC with similar needs | Counts per country from each registry or association; language and payment differences |
| **Face the diaspora** | The client's customers or investors live abroad (real estate, schools, healthcare, tourism) | The client's own enquiry and sales data by country |
| **Smaller niche, higher fee** | Few firms, but each large (private hospitals, international schools, SACCOs, lodges, law firms, private universities, developers) | Revenue proxies per firm; willingness to pay a premium monthly fee |
| **Association-led access** | A sector body offers events, directories, partner listings | Current membership access and data-sharing rules, confirmed with the body |

Confirm each association's current membership, event calendar and rules on
using member contact details before planning around it. Any list used for
outreach must be lawfully obtained: in Uganda, consent is required to collect
personal data and written objections to direct marketing must be honoured
within 14 days (register PL-01); in Kenya, direct marketing needs consent and a
free, simple opt-out, and objections are absolute (register PL-02).

## 6. Commit and review

Commit to the niche for twelve months. Review at the end of each quarter
against: named prospects added, conversations held, proof clients won, fee
achieved against floor. Do not change niche after two or three quiet months;
change the lead-generation method first.

## 7. Worked example (illustrative)

"Construction marketing" fails question 1: nobody calls themselves a
construction business when looking for a website agency. "Websites for Kampala
developers selling off-plan apartments to the diaspora" passes: developers name
themselves this way (1); they already buy billboards, social ads and portal
listings (2); the count is small in Uganda, so the agency adds Nairobi and
diaspora-facing work and prices higher (3, §5); and there is a developers'
association with events (4, to be confirmed). The founder previously worked in
property sales (tie-breaker). Transaction value is high: one sale covers years
of fees.
