# Niche Selection Rubric

**Purpose**: Decide whether the agency should specialise in a single vertical
("web design for X"), which vertical, and how to expand later — without
guessing. Encodes the specialisation frameworks of Blair Enns, Philip Morgan,
David C. Baker, April Dunford, and Seth Godin, calibrated for a Uganda / East
Africa premium agency.

**Read this when**: choosing or revising the agency niche, sanity-checking
whether a chosen niche is large enough to sustain the business, writing the
"we do X for Y" positioning sentence, or deciding when to add a second vertical.

This reference assumes you have read `agency-positioning/SKILL.md` and
`references/agency-narrative.md`. It sits upstream of the offer ladder: the
niche decision constrains every fee, proof asset, and proposal that follows.

---

## 1. The rule: specialise on at least one axis

A generalist agency competes on price and personal relationships. A specialist
competes on demonstrated expertise. Specialisation produces three measurable
advantages (Blair Enns, *The Win Without Pitching Manifesto*, 2010):

1. **Higher win rate** — fewer competitors are credible for the specific need.
2. **Price premium** — scarce, specific expertise commands more than commodity build.
3. **Power in the relationship** — the specialist leads; the client follows the diagnosis.

David C. Baker's default (2Bobs, "How to Make Horizontal Positioning Work"):
**"The default should be vertical because it's so easy to find your clients."**
For a city-based studio selling to brick-and-mortar SMBs, vertical is almost
always the correct axis.

The agency must be specific on **at least one** axis (industry OR problem OR
platform). "We build websites for everyone" is not a position.

## 2. The five specialisation types (Philip Morgan)

Choose one. They are listed easiest-to-validate last.

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Pure Vertical | One industry (or two closely related) | "Websites for beauty businesses" |
| Audience | A trait shared across industries | "Websites for women-led SMEs" |
| Pure Horizontal | One business problem across all industries | "We fix slow, unconvertible local sites" |
| Platform | Expertise on one stack | "Astro + headless CMS specialists" |
| Service | One narrowed offering as a low-risk test | "Website-in-a-week sprints" |

Default to **Pure Vertical** for a local studio. Use **Service** as a
low-commitment test if the founder is not yet ready to commit to one industry.

## 3. The market-size guardrail (decision gate)

Before committing to a vertical, count the addressable market. Morgan's
guardrail (attributed to David C. Baker's research):

- **Target pool: 1,000–10,000 prospect businesses** in your reachable market.
  - Fewer than 1,000 → the niche is too narrow; widen it (add an adjacent
    sub-sector or a second city).
  - More than 10,000 → too broad to dominate; narrow it (a sub-segment, or a
    smaller geography).
- **Competitor count: 10–100** credible competitors.
  - Fewer than 10 → likely insufficient demand (or you have mis-defined the niche).
  - More than 100 → red ocean; differentiate harder or pick a sub-niche.

Worked check — the Reddit example: "beauty businesses in our city, 3,000+
target businesses." 3,000 sits inside the 1,000–10,000 band → **passes the
guardrail.** A Kampala equivalent: count salons, spas, barbershops, nail bars,
and cosmetics retailers reachable in-person; if the count lands in-band, the
niche is viable.

Build the actual prospect list before you commit. Haus Advisors' viability
test: a vertical needs a **200+ company named prospect list** you can name and
reach to be workable. If you cannot list 200 real businesses, the niche is
theoretical.

## 4. Scoring shortlisted niches (Morgan)

When two or three niches pass the guardrail, score each:

- **Aggregate score = Impact + Profit + Interest** (rate each 1–5)
  - *Impact*: how much better can you make these businesses' outcomes?
  - *Profit*: can they pay premium fees? Is the work repeatable?
  - *Interest*: will the founder still care after 30 projects?
- **Risk score = 7 − Access − Credibility** (rate Access and Credibility 1–3; lower risk is better)
  - *Access*: can you reach decision-makers (in-person density, associations, events)?
  - *Credibility*: do you have proof or a believable story for this vertical?

Pick the niche with the highest Aggregate at acceptable Risk. Access is the
single most important factor for an in-person studio — see
`local-in-person-acquisition`.

## 5. Vertical vs horizontal decision

Choose **horizontal** (one problem across industries) only if all three hold
(Baker):

1. **Reachable** decision-makers (you can find and contact them efficiently).
2. **Pervasive** need (always relevant, not seasonal or one-off).
3. **Proprietary** differentiation (research, a method, or data competitors lack).

If any fails, default to vertical. Horizontal carries longer sales cycles and
weaker referral compounding for a young local studio.

## 6. The positioning statement formula

Write one sentence: **specific audience + expensive problem + distinct approach.**
It must pass the 12-second test (a stranger understands it) and the stranger
test (a stranger can repeat it). Ban buzzwords: "innovative", "holistic",
"strategic", "solutions" (see `glossary.md#banned-terms`).

Examples (structure, not copy to reuse verbatim):

- "We build booking-ready websites for salons and spas that turn Instagram traffic into appointments."
- "We give Kampala law firms a credible, compliant web presence that converts referrals into consultations."

State the problem as a business outcome (lost bookings, lost trust), not a
feature (no website, old design).

## 7. Concentration risk and its mitigation

A single-vertical agency is exposed if that vertical contracts (regulation,
seasonality, local downturn). Mitigate without abandoning the niche:

- **Vertical Core + Horizontal Edge.** Build the brand, marketing, and referral
  network around one or two industries of undisputed expertise. Take
  adjacent-industry work opportunistically — but do not rebuild the brand
  around it.
- **Reusable assets.** Design system, component library, intake forms, and SOPs
  (see `delivery-automation`) transfer across verticals, so a pivot costs less.
- **Referral cross-routing.** Referrals outside the niche go to partners or a
  sibling line, and the client is still rewarded — see
  `referral-program/references/cross-niche-and-partners.md`.

## 8. When and how to expand to a second niche

Expand only after **dominance in the first** — documented results, a visible
case-study corpus, and a referral flow that no longer depends on cold
prospecting. Then:

1. Pick an **adjacent** niche that reuses existing operational assets and
   creative logic (e.g. salons → wider wellness; law firms → wider professional
   services). Unrelated jumps dilute or kill the brand.
2. Model the second niche's economics separately — each vertical has its own
   ceiling and price tolerance.
3. Run the same guardrail (§3) and scoring (§4) on the new niche before
   committing marketing to it.
4. Prefer iterative expansion over a radical pivot; rapid repositioning causes
   "reputation thrashing" and resets accumulated authority.
