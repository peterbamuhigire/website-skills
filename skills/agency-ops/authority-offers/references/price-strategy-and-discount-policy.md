# Price Strategy And Discount Policy

Parent skill: [authority-offers](../SKILL.md). Read when setting or reviewing the price of an offer, writing a discount or promotion policy, diagnosing chronic under-pricing, or preparing a business to defend premium prices.

For how price is presented in sales copy, use `content-copy/long-form-sales-copy/references/offer-propositions-and-price-framing.md`. For the agency's own fee floors, use `agency-ops/agency-positioning/references/pricing-rules.md`.

## 1. Price-failure audit

Score the business against each failure (0 = absent, 1 = partly present, 2 = clearly present). Any score of 2 is enough to suppress profit.

| # | Failure | Evidence to look for |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pricing by formula or industry norm | "Everyone charges about this." |
| 2 | Excess concern with cheaper competitors | Prices set by the lowest rival. |
| 3 | Attracting price-first buyers | Heavy discount use; high churn; haggling. |
| 4 | Owner's fixed belief about what buyers will pay | Untested ceiling. |
| 5 | Allowing like-for-like comparison | Same package, same wording as rivals. |
| 6 | Weak differentiation | No named method, niche, or distinct guarantee. |
| 7 | No premium option | Nothing above the main offer. |
| 8 | Poor grasp of business arithmetic | Margin, acquisition cost, and lifetime value unknown. |
| 9 | Low business self-esteem | Apologising for price; discounting unprompted. |

Most price failure is attitudinal before it is tactical. Record the owner's beliefs about price alongside the numbers.

## 2. How prices get set

| Method | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Guesswork | Reject: no rationale. |
| Industry norm | Reject as a starting point: guarantees parity at best. |
| Client-dictated | Reject: hands control to the buyer. |
| Cost-plus | Reject as the main method: ignores value. Use only as a floor check. |
| Target return | Use: start from the required return and work backwards to price and volume. |

Prices must then evolve with objectives:

- **Sequential skimming:** launch high for early adopters, then step down deliberately.
- **Penetration pricing:** launch low to gain share. Use sparingly; it is hard to raise prices later.

Test the ceiling: raise prices in controlled steps and measure revenue, margin, and buyer quality, not only unit volume.

## 3. The under-pricing cascade

Explain this chain to owners who resist raising prices:

- Thin margin → little money for marketing → weak lead flow → desperate pricing → deeper discounts.
- Thin margin → cannot pay good staff → poor experience → complaints and churn → higher acquisition cost.
- Thin margin → no investment in improvement → the offer becomes a commodity.

The damage compounds before it is visible in the accounts.

## 4. Anti-commodity levers

There are no true commodities, only commodity thinking. Levers:

1. Sell the story, identity, and emotion around the product, not only the product.
2. Name the method, product, tiers, and customer rituals.
3. Position as a category of one so direct comparison fails.
4. Serve buyers who prefer fewer, better things.
5. Never try to win on price against a much larger low-cost competitor.

## 5. Niche and association

- Moving an offer from generic to a named niche (industry, profession, subculture) usually permits a higher price with no change in production cost. The deeper the audience's identity attachment, the stronger the effect. The niche version must genuinely serve that audience.
- Price is anchored by association. Build verifiable associations with respected clients, institutions, media, and venues.

## 6. Discount policy

### Rules

1. **Every discount needs a stated reason:** volume, prepayment, season, pre-order, behaviour change, or a charitable link. Never "just because".
2. **Discounts can lower perceived quality.** Buyers often rate the same product lower when they paid less. Protect flagship offers.
3. **Be unpredictable.** A sale that recurs on a fixed date trains buyers to wait. Vary timing, reason, and size.
4. **Isolate the damage.** If discounts are needed for acquisition, discount one designated entry product, not the whole range.
5. **Never give a prestige offer away free.** Add guarantees, inclusions, financing, service, or access instead.
6. **Always get something in exchange** (quid pro quo): early registration, referrals, multi-period booking, bulk purchase, or a charitable contribution linked to the purchase.
7. **Never discount without removing scope** in service businesses; offer a smaller complete scope instead.

### Free as a strategy

Free offers can:

- train buyers to expect more free;
- devalue what was previously paid for and upset past buyers;
- attract a list that rarely buys.

Use free for decision-making information and entry assets, not for the core paid deliverable.

## 7. Enough lead flow to say no

The strongest suppressor of premium pricing is desperation from too few prospects. Run more lead sources than needed at any time (referrals, speaking, content, partnerships, paid campaigns) so wrong-fit buyers can be declined. Never depend on a single introducer or channel.

## 8. Pre-eminence plan

Buyers least concerned with price seek the pre-eminent provider. Plan all three sides:

1. **Expertise and service:** advanced methods, trained team, proprietary process.
2. **High visibility:** publishing, speaking, events, media.
3. **Community contribution:** charitable, civic, or professional leadership.

## 9. Staff enforcement

Staff can quietly undo a price strategy:

- Remove unapproved authority to negotiate price down.
- Tie incentives to gross margin, not revenue alone.
- Script and rehearse every price conversation.
- Staff who cannot defend the price after training should not hold price conversations.

## 10. Several voices, one decider

Household and B2B purchases involve several voices (sceptic, budget holder, enthusiast, final decider). Map who holds each role, what each needs to hear, and close with the decider. Provide material the enthusiast can use to persuade the others.

## 11. Pricing in a downturn

The reflex is to cut prices. The usual better response: hold or raise prices, add value, shed wrong-fit customers, and market harder to the right ones. Downturns remove weaker competitors and can widen the premium gap. Confirm with the business's own margin and cash position before acting.

## 12. Playbook sequence

1. Run the price-failure audit.
2. Choose a niche for category-of-one positioning.
3. Build the three sides of pre-eminence.
4. Secure surplus lead flow.
5. Build the five-proposition stack (see the copy reference above).
6. Remove like-for-like comparison through bundling, naming, and method.
7. Set price from target return; test upwards.
8. Adopt the discount policy; require an exchange for every discount.
9. Protect prestige offers from free, sales, and discounts.
10. Train and enforce staff adherence.
11. Build verifiable associations.
12. Review pricing posture before reacting to a downturn.

## Regional application notes (East Africa)

Treat as hypotheses to verify before stating:

- Premium positioning is often more available than owners assume in major urban centres; check current market data before citing it.
- Private providers competing with public services (schools, clinics, security, savings groups) should make the hidden cost of the free option explicit.
- Niche framing (for example, single-origin specialty coffee versus generic export grade) can support a much higher price.
- Very low local-currency prices can signal counterfeit risk.
- In referral-driven markets, never rely on one introducer.
