# Social Service Pricing and Client Acquisition

Parent skill: [social-media](../SKILL.md). Read when choosing how to charge for social-media management, setting package prices, planning how a new social-media practice wins its first clients, or advising a client on ways to earn income from an audience.

The package tiers themselves (Launch, Grow, Scale) live in `legacy-guidance.md`. This file covers the pricing model decision, the price-setting procedure, first-client acquisition, and audience monetisation routes.

---

## 1. Choose the pricing model

| Model | Use when | Risk | Control |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hourly | New practice building a client base; small, uncertain scopes | Client tries to maximise hours or demands unlimited revisions | Cap revisions; log hours weekly; share the log |
| Monthly retainer | Ongoing management where strategy and relationship build over time | Client assumes they own your time and adds work | Written scope from the duty map; change requests priced separately |
| Per project | Account set-up, launch campaigns, one-off audits, or clients who will take over management | Under-estimating effort | Estimate effort first, add contingency, fix deliverables and acceptance criteria |

### Hourly revenue check
Monthly revenue = hours per client per month × hourly rate × number of clients.
Run this before committing to an hourly model to see whether the practice can reach its income target within available hours. If it cannot, move to retainers or projects.

### Rate bands
Do not quote salary or hourly benchmarks from memory. Check a current, dated local source for the market and experience level, and record the source and date in the pricing note. Calibrate Ugandan pricing with `../../agency-positioning/references/uganda-market-pricing-calibration.md`.

## 2. Set the price (procedure)

1. Consult the client first: what must social media achieve, and by when?
2. Identify their primary interest and the outcomes you have achieved for similar clients; price from the value of that outcome.
3. Research two or three comparable providers serving the same client type; note scope as well as price.
4. Present packages so new clients can self-select without a long explanation.
5. Listen to reactions to the price. Consistent "yes" without discussion suggests under-pricing; consistent refusal with no objection other than price suggests a positioning or targeting problem, not only a price problem.
6. Bill paid-media spend separately from management fees; state who pays the platform directly.
7. Agree scope, revision limits, response times, and approval steps in writing before starting.

## 3. Win the first clients

Work through these in order; each builds proof for the next.

1. **Decide honestly whether the work fits.** The practice must genuinely want to serve businesses through social media, including out-of-hours responsiveness.
2. **Build foundation knowledge.** Study relationship marketing, funnels, and the platforms' own training material.
3. **Build a portfolio.** Offer a small, time-boxed free or discounted engagement to one or two local businesses in exchange for permission to publish results as a case study.
4. **Make your own accounts exemplary.** Well structured, updated regularly, engaged. Quality and responsiveness matter more than follower count.
5. **Use a professional network where buyers are.** Join groups relevant to the target sector and contribute genuinely; a small operator can become the recognised specialist there.
6. **Attend local business events.** Breakfast meetings, chamber events, and sector associations. Present as "the social-media specialist for [sector/area]". See `../../local-in-person-acquisition/SKILL.md` for in-person routines.
7. **Offer a free health check.** Score the prospect's presence, send a written report, and propose the fix. Procedure: `social-role-scoping-and-account-health-check.md` section 5.
8. **Run a free seminar.** A short session for local business owners on social media for business; give practical take-aways, collect consented contact details, and offer a paid workshop or strategy session afterwards. A small room is a sound start.
9. **Partner with complementary firms.** SEO firms, web designers, PR firms, and media buyers often have clients needing social work. Agree whether you deliver under your brand or theirs.
   - Check the agency's own positioning first: `../../agency-positioning/references/agency-narrative.md` states the house agency does not white-label. Offer unbranded delivery only where the positioning allows it.
10. **Document every success.** After each result, write a case study and publish it on your own channels. Template: `../../agency-positioning/references/case-study-template.md`.

## 4. Audience monetisation routes

Use when a client (creator, consultant, or media-led business) asks how an audience earns money. Choose one primary route and one secondary route; more dilutes effort.

| Route | How it works | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Affiliate promotion | Recommend third-party products for commission | Write considered reviews rather than posting bare links; disclose every commercial relationship clearly; check local advertising and platform disclosure rules |
| Own digital products | Guides, courses, templates, audio programmes | Needs a proven topic and a delivery platform; price with `../../authority-offers/SKILL.md` |
| Freelance management services | Manage accounts for businesses | Use sections 1–3 above |
| Coaching or consulting | Teach businesses to run their own social media | Suits clients who will not pay for ongoing management |
| Paid webinars or workshops | Topic-specific sessions charged per participant | Grow from free seminars once demand is evident |
| Sponsorship | Brands pay for sponsored posts once the audience is substantial and relevant | Label sponsored content; keep editorial control consistent with the character sheet |
